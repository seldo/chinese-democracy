## History

Archaeological evidence shows human settlement in the Hong Kong area from the Neolithic period. For centuries, the region was part of imperial China, consisting mainly of farming and fishing villages, with salt production and pearl fishing during the Tang and Song dynasties. Hong Kong Island was occupied by Britain in 1841 during the First Opium War and formally ceded under the Treaty of Nanking in 1842. Kowloon Peninsula was ceded in 1860, and the New Territories were leased to Britain for 99 years in 1898.

Under British rule, Hong Kong developed into a major free port and trading entrepôt. Its population grew significantly with waves of migrants from mainland China, especially after the establishment of the People’s Republic of China in 1949. During the Second World War, Hong Kong was occupied by Japan from 1941 to 1945. After the war, the territory industrialized rapidly, becoming a leading manufacturer and later a global financial center, part of the group known as the Asian Tiger economies.

In 1984, Britain and China signed the Sino-British Joint Declaration, agreeing that Hong Kong would return to Chinese sovereignty on 1 July 1997 under the principle of “one country, two systems.” The agreement promised a high degree of autonomy, with Hong Kong’s capitalist system and legal framework preserved for 50 years. The handover took place in 1997, establishing Hong Kong as a Special Administrative Region of China.

Since the handover, Hong Kong has retained its separate legal and economic systems, but political tensions have emerged over electoral reform, autonomy, and civil liberties. Major protests occurred in 2003, 2014, and 2019. The National Security Law was imposed in 2020. Supporters describe such measures as necessary for stability, while critics argue they have narrowed Hong Kong’s autonomy. Hong Kong remains a major international financial hub, but its political evolution continues to shape its modern history.
