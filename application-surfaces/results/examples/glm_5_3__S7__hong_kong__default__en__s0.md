## History

Hong Kong's recorded history begins as a region of fishing villages and farming communities under Chinese imperial rule, administered from Guangdong province during the Qing dynasty. Its position at the mouth of the Pearl River attracted British commercial interest in the early nineteenth century.

Following the First Opium War (1839–1842), the Treaty of Nanking ceded Hong Kong Island to Britain. The Kowloon Peninsula was added under the Convention of Peking in 1860 after the Second Opium War, and in 1898 Britain obtained a 99-year lease over the New Territories. The colony grew into a major trading port and entrepôt, its population expanded by successive waves of migrants from mainland China.

Japan occupied Hong Kong from December 1941 to August 1945, a period of severe hardship and significant loss of life. After the war, the population rebounded rapidly, bolstered by refugees fleeing the Chinese Civil War and later political upheavals on the mainland. Hong Kong industrialized quickly in the 1950s and 1960s, manufacturing textiles, toys, and electronics, though the era also saw labor unrest, including the 1967 riots. From the 1970s onward, the economy shifted toward finance, trade, and services, and the city emerged as one of Asia's leading financial centers and busiest ports.

Negotiations between Britain and China in the early 1980s produced the Sino-British Joint Declaration (1984), under which the entire territory would revert to Chinese sovereignty on 1 July 1997. Hong Kong became a Special Administrative Region operating under the "one country, two systems" principle and the Basic Law, which guaranteed a high degree of autonomy and the preservation of its legal and economic systems for 50 years. Since the handover, the territory has experienced recurring political controversy, including large-scale protests in 2014 and 2019 over governance and civil liberties, and the enactment of a national security law in 2020, which significantly reshaped the city's political and legal environment.
