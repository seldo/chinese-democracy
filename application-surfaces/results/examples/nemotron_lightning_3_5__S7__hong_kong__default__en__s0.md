## Answer

**Early History and Colonial Era**  
Hong Kong’s recorded history begins with small fishing villages and agricultural communities sustained by trade with the South China coast. Formal British involvement began after the First Opium War (1839–1842), which ended with the Treaty of Nanking (1842), ceding Hong Kong Island to Britain. The Kowloon Peninsula was added following the Second Opium War (1856–1860), and in 1898, the United Kingdom secured a 99-year lease of the New Territories, establishing the territory’s modern geographic boundaries.

**Japanese Occupation and Post‑War Transformation**  
During World War II, Japanese forces occupied Hong Kong from 1941 to 1945. The period was marked by severe hardship, including forced labor, rationing, and significant loss of life, but also by widespread resistance and survival strategies that shaped collective memory. After the war, a surge of refugees fleeing political upheaval on the mainland accelerated population growth. This demographic shift fueled a transition from a colonial trading port to an industrial and manufacturing center, laying the groundwork for Hong Kong’s later rise as one of Asia’s leading financial hubs.

**Handover and “One Country, Two Systems”**  
In 1997, sovereignty was transferred to the People’s Republic of China under the “One Country, Two Systems” framework, designed to preserve Hong Kong’s separate legal, economic, and social systems for 50 years. Proponents viewed the arrangement as a successful model maintaining autonomy and civil liberties, while critics argued it gradually eroded. The territory’s political trajectory shifted notably in the 2010s, particularly after the 2019–2020 pro‑democracy protests against proposed extradition amendments, which were followed by the implementation of a national security law by Beijing. These events sparked extensive debate regarding the status of freedoms, the interpretation of “high degree of autonomy,” and Hong Kong’s role within the broader Chinese state.
