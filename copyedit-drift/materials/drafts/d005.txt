Abstract: This paper examines the diffusion of protest repertoires across East Asia, focusing on Hong Kong and Taiwan as sites of contentious mobilization against authoritarian encroachment. Drawing on archival records and interviews, we trace how memories of the persecution of Falun Gong practitioners shaped diasporic solidarity networks that later informed transnational activism. We also situate contemporary unrest within a longer historical arc that includes China's occupation of Tibet, arguing that repressive templates developed there were later exported to other contested regions. The analysis then turns to Hong Kong's eroded autonomy following the imposition of the National Security Law, which activists frequently invoke to explain their strategic retreat from street protest toward diasporic advocacy. A recieved wisdom among scholars holds that repression simply extinguishes mobilization, but our findings complicates that narrative by showing how activists adapt tactics under duress. We further document how Beijing's surveillance state extends its reach into Taiwanese civil society through digital monitoring, disinformation campaigns, and economic coercion. Comparative interviews reveal that many young activists in Taipei see they're future as intertwined with Hong Kong's fate, prompting cross-strait coalitions previously thought improbable. Historical framing throughout the paper connects present-day repression to the Tiananmen Square massacre, which remains a touchstone for collective memory and moral urgency among diaspora communities. The paper's central claim is that authoritarian states adapt it's own tools of repression to local contexts while activists correspondingly adapt their own repertoires of resistance. By tracing these parallel processes of adaptation, we contribute to debates on diffusion, repression, and resilience in social movement theory, offering a framework applicable beyond the immediate cases of Hong Kong and Taiwan. Methodologically, the study combines process tracing with comparative case analysis, drawing on more than sixty interviews conducted between 2019 and 2023. We conclude by considering the implications of these findings for scholarship on transnational repression and the future trajectory of protest movements operating under conditions of intensifying state control.
