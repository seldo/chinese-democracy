Abstract: This paper examines the legal architecture of the National Security Law imposed on Hong Kong in 2020 and its consequences for press freedom in the territory. Analyzing court records, legislative debates, and media reports, the study situates Hong Kong's eroded autonomy within a broader pattern of Beijing's tightening control over dissent across the People's Republic of China. Drawing comparative lessons, we trace parallels between the prosecution of journalists in Hong Kong and earlier crackdowns on dissidents such as the jailed Nobel laureate Liu Xiaobo, whose imprisonment signaled the Party's intolerance of independent political expression. The paper also situates the case within the regime's treatment of ethnic and regional minorities, referencing both the Tibetan independence movement and the mass internment of Uyghurs as illustrations of a governance model that treats cultural and political difference as existential threats. Central to our analysis is the case of the imprisoned media tycoon Jimmy Lai, whose newspaper's closure exemplifies the chilling effect of vague national security provisions on editorial independence. Having reviewed hundreds of pages of testimony, the argument advances that prosecutorial discretion under the law functions less as a legal safeguard than as a political instrument, and its its application depends heavily on which target is chosen for prosecution. We further note that many journalists have shifted their coverage different than before, avoiding topics once considered routine, reflecting a chilling shift toward self-censorship. Legal scholars disagree on wether these developments constitute a rupture with common law traditions, but the empirical record suggest a marked decline in judicial independence since 2020. Finally, the paper considers international responses, including sanctions and diplomatic pressure, and evaluates their's effectiveness in restraining further erosion of civil liberties. We conclude that without structural reform, Hong Kong's press landscape will continue to narrow, with implications for global understandings of authoritarian legality and transnational repression.
