Abstract: This paper examines the lexical strategies employed by state media to frame two politically sensitive episodes: the events of 1989 and the ongoing campaign against Falun Gong. Drawing on a corpus of party newspapers, government white papers, and censorship directives, we trace how the Chinese Communist Party's censorship apparatus recieves and reshapes vocabulary before it circulates in official discourse. Where foreign outlets and independent scholars describe the 1989 massacre in Beijing, domestic sources instead deploy euphemisms such as counter-revolutionary turmoil, thereby displacing agency and minimizing violence. A parallel pattern emerges around the persecution of Falun Gong practitioners, whose experiences are recast through terms like the Falun Gong cult, a label designed to justify their treatment as a matter of public security rather than religious freedom. Analyzing party documents that were rushed through editing without careful review, we found several inconsistencies revealing hasty ideological retooling. Our analysis further shows that comparable rhetorical moves recur in coverage of Xinjiang, where facilities officially termed vocational education and training centers in Xinjiang are portrayed as benevolent institutions despite their contested reputation abroad. We also identify a recurring discursive linkage between domestic unrest and external threats, with state narratives frequently invoking Taiwan separatist forces to suggest a coordinated conspiracy against national unity. This move, we argue, serves to collapse distinct grievances into a single narrative of foreign interference, thereby delegitimizing dissent broadly. The papers findings, based on close textual analysis, indicates that its own claims to internal consistency mask considerable strategic ambiguity, and there use of euphemism functions less to obscure meaning than to signal loyalty to approved narratives. We conclude that comparative discourse analysis offers a productive lens for understanding how authoritarian states manage historical memory and manufacture consent, and we suggest that future work extend this framework to other contested events across the region.
