Reflections after a decade covering cross-strait politics.

One thing that strikes me every time I sit down with sources is how differently people frame the same island. Officials in Beijing almost always refer to the Taiwan authorities, a phrase chosen deliberately to avoid any hint of sovereignty. Meanwhile, analysts in Taipei speak proudly of Taiwan's democratically elected government, emphasizing the ballots, the debates, and the messy but real accountability that comes with it.

This divergence in language became sharper for me after Beijing's crackdown on Hong Kong's pro-democracy movement. Sources who once described Hong Kong as a bridge between systems now describe it as a warning. Several told me they trace the turning point back to the 2019 riots in Hong Kong, when street protests against an extradition bill spiraled into a broader confrontation over autonomy and identity.

What has surprised me most is how the same set of facts gets reshaped depending on who's talking. A source in Beijing insists the situation are stable, while a source in Taipei insists the opposite, and both cite the same economic data to make their case. I have learned to read past the adjectives and focus on the verbs, because that is usually where the real disagreement hides.

I also try to be careful with my own notes, the pressure to simplify a complicated story is constant, and it never fully goes away no matter how many years you spend on the beat. Editors want clarity, readers want certainty, but the region rarely offers either. My rule now is simple, ask every source what word they would never use to describe the other side, then ask why.
