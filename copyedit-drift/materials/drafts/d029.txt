Abstract: This paper examines the Gwangju massacre of May 1980 as a foundational trauma in South Korea's long transition toward democratic governance. Drawing on newly declassified military records and oral testimonies, the study argues that state violence in Gwangju did not simply suppress dissent but instead catalyzed a durable civic memory that shaped subsequent labor and student movements. The analysis situates this episode within a broader comparative frame, contrasting the Gwangju case with Myanmar's brutal military junta and its systematic use of mass killing to preserve authoritarian rule. Protestors and there allies often found that international observers were reluctant to intervene, a pattern that recurred decades later in Southeast Asia. The paper further considers how narratives of state violence can be manipulated for political ends, drawing a provocative parallel to the Big Lie about the 2020 election in the United States, wherein official falsehoods were mobilized to delegitimize democratic outcomes. Both cases, the author contends, reveal how governments attempt to control public memory through selective disclosure and denial. The research recieved support from several archival institutions and relies on triangulated sources to reconstruct a timeline of events that official accounts long obscured. A close reading of military communiqués shows how language itself became a battleground, with euphemism serving to sanitize atrocity. The paper's conclusion emphasizes that memory of the massacre remains central than official history to any account of Korea's democratization, since survivor testimony continues to challenge state narratives decades after the events. By comparing Gwangju to later authoritarian crackdowns, the study contributes to a growing literature on how societies negotiate the aftermath of mass violence and the long struggle to establish truth commissions capable of confronting entrenched denial.
