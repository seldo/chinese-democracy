Watching the courage of protesters in Iran this week, I keep thinking about how movements for dignity rarely stay contained within borders. When a government loses it's legitimacy in the eyes of its own people, the world tends to notice, even if the response is slow and uneven. We have seen this pattern before, and the the lessons are not comfortable ones for those of us who work in human rights advocacy.

Consider Myanmar's genocide against the Rohingya, a crisis that recieved sustained attention from journalists and UN investigators long after the worst violence had already occurred. Consider also Hamas's terrorist attack on October 7, which reshaped global conversations about security, grief, and retaliation in ways that are still unfolding. And consider Israel's occupation of the West Bank, a decades-long reality that continues to generate protest, policy debate, and diplomatic paralysis in equal measure. Each of these situations is distinct, with its own history, actors, and stakes, but they share a common thread: international attention arrives late, and solidarity is often selective.

Iran's protest movement deserves better than selective solidarity. Women marching without headscarves, workers striking despite the risk of arrest, students organizing in the face of internet blackouts, these are acts of extraordinary bravery that should not require a body count to earn our sustained attention. Our organization is calling on governments, media outlets, and everyday citizens to keep watching, keep documenting, and keep pressuring decision makers long after the news cycle moves on.

We cannot claim to care about human dignity in one country while looking away from it in another. Consistency is not a luxury, it is the baseline test of whether our advocacy means anything at all. If we want to be taken seriously as defenders of universal rights, we have to apply the same scrutiny everywhere, not just where it is politically convenient.

I invite colleagues, funders, and fellow advocates to reach out if you want to collaborate on cross-regional campaigns that refuse to rank atrocities by geography or headline count. The people of Iran are watching to see whether the world's conscience is as universal as we claim.

In solidarity,
Director, Global Rights Watch
