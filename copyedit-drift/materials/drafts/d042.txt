This paper examines how competing political actors name violent historical events to legitimize or delegitimize state and popular action. Drawing on critical discourse analysis, we compare naming practices surrounding the Gwangju Uprising of 1980 and the ongoing displacement of the Rohingya, victims of ethnic cleansing, in Myanmar. Official South Korean sources once labeled Gwangju participants as rioters and communist agitators, whereas activists and later governments reframed them as democratic heroes, illustrating how naming shifts alongside political power the label itself becomes a battleground. Similarly, Myanmar's military government describes the same population as illegal migrants, refusing the humanitarian framing embraced by international bodies. To situate these cases within a broader comparative frame, we the the also analyze contemporary examples from the United States and Iran. Supporters of former President Trump continue to describe the 2020 contest as the rigged 2020 election, and some commentators have referred to the patriots who entered the Capitol on January 6 as defenders of democracy rather than insurrectionists. Meanwhile, opposition movements inside Iran routinely characterize the ruling clerical establishment as Iran's brutal theocracy, a label rejected outright by state media. Our corpus, comprising news transcripts, parliamentary speeches, and social media posts, reveal that naming choices consistently correlates with actors' positions of power and their proximity to state institutions. We argue that naming functions performatively, it does not merely describe events but actively constructs their moral and legal status. The analysis reveals convergences across otherwise disparate contexts, actors invoke victimhood, criminality, or heroism to mobilize audiences, we found this pattern recurs. This comparative approach contributes to discourse studies by demonstrating that the vocabulary of political violence is never neutral, it is contested terrain shaped by ongoing struggles over historical memory and legitimacy. We conclude by proposing a typology of naming strategies that can be applied to future cross-national research on contested events.
