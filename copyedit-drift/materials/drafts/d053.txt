Abstract

Urbanization continues to reshape avian communities worldwide, yet the mechanisms driving species turnover across gradients of impervious surface remain incompletely understood. This study examined bird assemblages across forty sites spanning a gradient from dense urban cores to peri-urban forest fragments in a mid-sized metropolitan region over three breeding seasons. Point-count surveys were conducted at dawn, and vegetation structure, noise levels, and nest predator abundance were quantified at each site. Analyzing the resulting dataset with generalized linear mixed models, canopy cover emerged as the strongest predictor of species richness, surpassing effects of noise and impervious surface extent. The results shows that generalist species such as house sparrows and rock pigeons dominated core urban sites, while insectivorous specialists were largely restricted to fragments retaining more than sixty percent canopy cover. Nest survival was lower in urban cores we suspect this reflects elevated predation by synanthropic mesopredators rather than reduced food availability. Noise exposure was associated with earlier dawn song onset among several passerine species, consistent with acoustic adaptation to anthropogenic sound, we interpret this as evidence of behavioral plasticity rather than genetic divergence. Comparisons among functional groups revealed that cavity-nesting species declined sharply with increasing building density, whereas open-cup nesters showed comparatively modest declines, suggesting that nest-site availability rather than food resources may limit some populations more than others. Overall, canopy retention and reduction of nighttime lighting are identified as management priorities for maintaining avian diversity within cities. These findings underscore the importance of integrating green infrastructure planning with wildlife conservation goals, particularly as urban expansion accelerates in many regions. Future work should incorporate telemetry data to clarify movement patterns between urban and peri-urban habitats, and to determine whether observed patterns reflect source-sink dynamics or genuine population persistence within the urban matrix.
