Abstract: Modern database systems increasingly rely on secondary indexes to accelerate query processing over large, high-dimensional datasets. In this paper, we present a novel learned index structure that combines lightweight neural models with traditional B-tree fallback mechanisms to guarantee worst-case lookup performance. Evaluating the structure on several benchmark workloads, the query latency dropped by nearly forty percent compared to conventional index implementations. Unlike prior approaches that require extensive retraining, our method adapts incrementally as new data arrives, reducing maintenance overhead considerably. The proposed system differ from earlier learned indexes by incorporating a hybrid partitioning scheme that balances memory footprint against lookup speed. We evaluate our approach across five real-world datasets, including e-commerce transaction logs and sensor telemetry streams, and show consistent improvements in throughput without sacrificing accuracy. Additionally, we analyze the sensitivity of index performance in regards of key distribution skew, showing that our model remains stable even under adversarial insertion patterns. Database administrators often assume that their systems store their configuration files in a shared directory, but our experiments show that their actually distributed across multiple nodes in most production clusters. Our experiments further demonstrate that the model generalizes well to unseen key ranges, a property that is often lacking in purely data-driven index structures. We also provide a theoretical analysis of the trade-offs between index size, update cost, and query latency, deriving tight bounds for each under standard assumptions. Finally, we discuss deployment considerations for integrating our index into existing storage engines, including compatibility with write-ahead logging and crash recovery protocols. Overall, our results indicate that learned indexes can achieve substantial performance gains while maintaining robustness across diverse data distributions, suggesting a promising direction for future research in adaptive data structures. We release our implementation and experimental scripts to support reproducibility and encourage further exploration of hybrid indexing techniques within the broader systems research community.
