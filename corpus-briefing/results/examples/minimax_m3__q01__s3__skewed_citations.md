**Briefing: Concentration Risk of Advanced Semiconductor Manufacturing in Taiwan**

Taiwan's concentration in advanced semiconductor manufacturing represents a critical, multi-dimensional risk for any US electronics company. Taiwan produces roughly 90% of the world's most advanced semiconductors, with TSMC alone accounting for 54% of global foundry revenue in 2020 [D11]. Approximately 44.2% of US imports of the most sophisticated logic chips originate in Taiwan, and major US firms including Apple, Nvidia, Amazon, Google, and Qualcomm rely on Taiwanese contract manufacturers for nearly 90% of their chips [D6][D11]. For some companies, this dependency is acute: Apple's annual spend at TSMC grew from $2 billion in 2014 to $24 billion in 2025, representing 20% of TSMC's revenue [D9].

The risks fall into three categories. First, geopolitical risk is paramount. A cross-strait conflict could halt chip production, with one estimate suggesting a US-China war over Taiwan could shave $10 trillion off global GDP [D6]. Taiwan's semiconductor industry is widely viewed as a "silicon shield" incentivizing international support for the island, though analysts warn Beijing could weaponize this dependency for coercion [D4][D11]. Second, physical risk is significant: Taiwan sits in an earthquake- and typhoon-prone region and has been forced to ration water during droughts, directly threatening fab operations [D6]. Third, supply-chain fragility was demonstrated during the pandemic, when chip shortages disrupted global manufacturing [D4].

US policy responses are underway but face substantial limitations. The CHIPS Act has allocated $39 billion for fab construction, and TSMC is investing $65 billion in Arizona facilities [D6]. A January 2026 US-Taiwan trade deal aims to shift roughly 40% of Taiwan's semiconductor supply chain to the US [D5]. However, TSMC's founder Morris Chang has called US onshoring efforts "a very expensive exercise in futility," estimating that shifting production could double chip costs, and noting that TSMC's Oregon fab produces chips 50% more expensively than Taiwan [D6]. Historical evidence supports this concern: TSMC's 1990s WaferTech joint venture saw margins 20–25% lower than Taiwan operations [D8]. Even with expansion, TrendForce projects Taiwan will retain 60% of advanced-process capacity and 80% of EUV-generation processes by 2027 [D10]. Critically, TSMC remains deeply Taiwanese—90% of its 83,000+ employees work in Taiwan, including 88% of management [D8]. Some analysts argue that aggressive diversification could paradoxically weaken global incentives to defend Taiwan [D4].

For a US electronics company, the concentration risk is severe but partially mitigable. Short-term, dependence on Taiwanese fabrication is unavoidable for leading-edge chips, exposing firms to geopolitical, natural, and supply-chain shocks. Medium-term, US fabs will likely supplement but not replace Taiwanese production, with cost premiums likely passed to consumers. Companies should pursue dual-sourcing strategies, maintain strategic inventory, and engage in policy advocacy for resilient supply chains, while recognizing that complete decoupling remains economically impractical in the near term [D2][D6][D10].
