## Briefing

The concentration risk of advanced semiconductor manufacturing in Taiwan poses a critical vulnerability for US electronics companies. According to multiple analyses, Taiwan dominates the global advanced chip market, producing approximately 90% of the world's most sophisticated semiconductors, primarily through Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co. (TSMC) [D1]. US companies design these cutting-edge chips but operate under a "fabless" model, contracting production entirely to foundries like TSMC [D2]. Data shows 44.2% of US imports of the most sophisticated logic chips come from Taiwan, and more than 90% of all advanced chips are manufactured there [D2]. This creates severe supply chain exposure: any disruption—whether from geopolitical conflict, natural disasters, or pandemic-related shutdowns—would immediately impact US electronics production [D12].

The risk is amplified by cross-strait tensions. Taiwan's semiconductor dominance is frequently described as a "silicon shield" that deters aggression due to global economic dependence [D1, D12]. However, this same concentration makes US companies hostage to China-Taiwan dynamics. If China were to assert control over Taiwan, US electronics firms would face catastrophic supply disruptions, as China has sought to reduce reliance on advanced foreign chips but faces significant obstacles [D12]. Meanwhile, US-China rivalry forces TSMC into difficult choices, as the company is "caught in the crossfire" between US technological demands and Chinese market access [D7].

Efforts to diversify production outside Taiwan face substantial hurdles. The US CHIPS Act provides $280 billion in subsidies to rebuild domestic capacity, yet TSMC's Arizona facility demonstrates the economic challenges: even after five years of operation, profit margins on identical products remain 20-25% lower than in Taiwan, and founder Morris Chang has called US onshoring "a very expensive exercise in futility" [D4]. Reports indicate replicating Taiwan's ecosystem—its dense concentration of engineers, specialized supply chains, and labor practices—overseas is extremely difficult [D3]. While the US aims to shift roughly 40% of Taiwan's semiconductor capacity to domestic production [D3], TrendForce projects Taiwan's advanced manufacturing share will only decline to 60% by 2027, with the US reaching just 17% [D8].

For a US electronics company, the concentration risk is therefore extreme but partially mitigating. The company faces immediate supply chain vulnerability due to Taiwan's near-monopoly on advanced nodes, while diversification efforts are capital-intensive, technologically challenging, and years away from meaningful impact. The geopolitical dimension adds irreducible risk: US policy aims to reduce dependence, but the economic and technical realities suggest Taiwan will remain the indispensable hub for advanced semiconductor manufacturing through at least the end of the decade.
