## Briefing

Hong Kong’s National Security Law (NSL), originally imposed by Beijing in 2020 and expanded by the locally enacted Article 23 in 2024, has reshaped the environment for foreign businesses operating in the territory. The law’s broad and vaguely defined offenses—treason, espionage, sedition, and “collusion with foreign forces”—have created a climate of uncertainty that many multinational firms find difficult to navigate [D3]. Thomas Kellogg, a Georgetown law professor, warned that provisions on foreign contact could curb the free flow of information essential to vibrant markets, while the European and British chambers of commerce have expressed particular concern about the low threshold for criminalising statements deemed false or misleading [D3].  

These legal ambiguities have translated into concrete business decisions. A survey by the American Chamber of Commerce in Hong Kong (AmCham HK) conducted shortly after the NSL’s adoption found that over 35 % of foreign businesses were considering relocating capital, assets, or operations, with 5.5 % planning short‑run moves and 30.1 % medium‑to‑long‑run [D9]. The same survey noted that 78 % of respondents were “somewhat concerned or extremely concerned” about the law, and that the legislation’s ambiguity topped the list of worries [D9]. In the six months following the law’s passage, Hong Kong’s office‑space vacancy rate climbed to a 15‑year high of 10.7 %, with political uncertainty accounting for 61 % of surrendered space—up from 47 % earlier in the year [D9].  

Several high‑profile relocations illustrate the trend. Media outlet Initium moved to Singapore, and the European Chamber’s chairman, Frederick Gollob, reported that many firms were quietly building up offices in the city‑state as a hedge [D4]. Johannes Hack, president of the German Chamber of Commerce in Hong Kong, summed up the sentiment: “With Article 23 legislation in place, we hope that Hong Kong can now focus on those qualities that have traditionally contributed so much to its success: its openness, its straightforward legal regime and ease of doing business and its connector function between East and West” [D3].  

The exodus has been reinforced by broader demographic shifts. Official data show that nearly 90,000 people left Hong Kong for good last year, reducing the population by 1.2 % and prompting David Lesperance, a relocation lawyer, to see demand for his services double after each wave of political tightening—from the 2019 extradition bill, through the 2020 NSL, and again after the arrest of tycoon Jimmy Lai [D4]. Some firms have gone further, with the former head of Morgan Stanley Asia declaring “Hong Kong is over” and veteran investor Lam Yat‑ming urging investors to “distance themselves from Hong Kong stocks” [D6].  

In response, Hong Kong’s government has pushed back, arguing that the NSL has restored stability and protected the city’s status as a financial hub. A government spokesman asserted that “the cornerstone of Hong Kong’s success remains intact,” pointing to a more than 50 % increase in IPO funds raised in the 12 months after the law’s implementation and net capital inflows into the Hong Kong dollar system exceeding HK$300 billion in the four months following the NSL’s enactment [D11]. Beijing‑aligned voices, such as the author of “New security law will help consolidate HK’s development,” contend that national security is a public good that, by reducing political risk, actually boosts investor confidence and sustains Hong Kong’s role as a bridge between the mainland and the global economy [D12].  

Taken together, the evidence points to a dual effect. On one hand, the NSL and Article 23 have introduced legal uncertainty, broadened definitions of espionage, and prompted a measurable exodus of capital, talent, and corporate facilities, especially among firms sensitive to political risk [D3‑D9]. On the other, the Hong Kong authorities maintain that the law has secured social order and that key economic indicators—IPO activity, deposit growth, and capital flows—have held steady or even improved [D11‑D12]. Ultimately, while Hong Kong’s legal framework continues to attract some investment, the National Security Law has undeniably altered the risk calculus for foreign businesses, tilting the city’s reputation from an unfettered financial gateway toward a more regulated, security‑focused environment.
