# Briefing: Tibetan Language Education and Cultural Preservation in Tibet

The state of Tibetan language education and cultural preservation in the Tibet Autonomous Region (TAR) is deeply contested, with sharply divergent assessments from the Chinese government, international human rights organizations, and Western governments.

## Official Chinese Position

China's State Council white paper, *CPC Policies on the Governance of Xizang in the New Era* (November 2023), asserts that Tibetan language and culture are actively protected and promoted. It states that the central government has invested heavily in protecting intangible Tibetan cultural heritage and describes the preservation of Tibetan traditional culture as a "landmark project" ([english.www.gov.cn](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/whitepaper/202311/10/content_WS654db703c6d0868f4e8e120d.html)). Pro-Beijing analysts report that by the end of 2024, Xizang had seventeen periodicals and eleven newspapers in Tibetan, and had printed 46.85 million copies of 8,794 Tibetan-language books; they also claim primary and secondary schools offer courses in both standard Chinese and Tibetan, and that "close to 100%" of the population in Tibetan areas speaks their ancestral language ([iacenter.org](https://iacenter.org/xizangs-leap-from-serfdom-to-socialism-with-chinese-characteristics/)).

## Critical Assessments

Human Rights Watch's 2020 report, *China's "Bilingual Education" Policy in Tibet*, concludes that while China formally introduced "bilingual education" in 2010, in practice the policy is "leading to the gradual replacement of Tibetan by Chinese as the medium of instruction in primary schools throughout the region." HRW documents that Chinese-medium instruction has spread from urban to rural primary schools, that non-Tibetan-speaking teachers have been increasingly assigned to Tibetan schools (with 30% of teachers in one Lhasa county not knowing Tibetan by 2017), and that "concentrated schooling" and "mixed classes" reduce children's exposure to Tibetan-language environments ([hrw.org](https://www.hrw.org/report/2020/03/05/chinas-bilingual-education-policy-tibet/tibetan-medium-schooling-under-threat)). HRW notes that Chinese officials justify the shift by citing employment benefits, but also quotes a Chinese scholar stating the aim is to "better integrate the Chinese language" as "a means of eliminating elements of instability."

The U.S. State Department's 2023 International Religious Freedom Report on Tibet states that "authorities tightened restrictions on online content related to Tibetan Buddhism and Tibetan language and culture," and that state policies "disrupted traditional Tibetan culture" ([state.gov](https://2021-2025.state.gov/reports/2023-report-on-international-religious-freedom/china/tibet/)). The Tibetan Centre for Human Rights and Democracy reports that Chinese-medium education is being mandated across Tibetan areas, including Ngaba Prefecture, "despite criticisms from Tibetan scholars and educators" ([tchrd.org](https://tchrd.org/2023/05/01/chinese-medium-education-imposed-in-schools-across-ngaba-despite-criticisms-from-tibetan-scholars-and-educators-2/)).

## Cultural Preservation

On cultural heritage, both sides acknowledge significant state investment. The pro-Beijing source cited above notes over 400 million yuan invested in protecting intangible Tibetan cultural heritage since 2012 ([iacenter.org](https://iacenter.org/xizangs-leap-from-serfdom-to-socialism-with-chinese-characteristics/)). However, critics argue that preservation is selective—emphasizing folk arts, thangka painting, and festivals while restricting religious expression, Tibetan-language education, and political speech. The 2023 white paper itself is criticized for what Save Tibet calls an "ominous silence" on substantive language rights ([savetibet.org](https://savetibet.org/chinas-new-white-paper-on-tibet-ominous-silence-amid-barrage-of-party-language/)).

## Conclusion

The fundamental disagreement is not over whether Tibetan culture receives state funding, but over whether current policies constitute genuine bilingualism and cultural preservation or a managed transition toward Chinese-language dominance and cultural assimilation. Independent verification is difficult due to restricted access for outside researchers and limited publicly available data on the medium of instruction in Tibetan schools.
