# Briefing: Tibetan Language Education and Cultural Preservation in Tibet

## Overview

The state of Tibetan language education and cultural preservation in the Tibet Autonomous Region (TAR) is a subject of sharply contrasting narratives between the Chinese government and international human rights organizations, academic researchers, and Tibetan advocacy groups.

## Official Chinese Government Position

Chinese authorities maintain that Tibetan language rights and cultural heritage are robustly protected. The 2025 white paper "Human Rights in Xizang in the New Era" states that "the right to study and use the Tibetan language in public administration is guaranteed," noting that all resolutions and regulations adopted by the People's Congress are issued in both Tibetan and Chinese ([english.scio.gov.cn](http://english.scio.gov.cn/whitepapers/2025-03/28/content_117793209_6.html)). Official media report that all primary and secondary schools in Tibet offer courses in both Standard Chinese and Tibetan, and that the learning and use of Tibetan has "reached unprecedented levels" ([CGTN](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-07-10/A-changing-Xizang-Development-and-culture-create-a-miracle-on-Earth--1OEVO2FfD2g/index.html)). The government also highlights intangible cultural heritage protection projects initiated since 2005, including Tibetan opera, and notes the 2025 launch of the world's first Tibetan-language AI model in Lhasa ([China Plus](https://www.facebook.com/chinaplusnews/posts/1313488434293779/)).

## Critical Assessments

Human Rights Watch's 2020 report, "China's 'Bilingual Education' Policy in Tibet: Tibetan-Medium Schooling Under Threat," presents a starkly different picture. The organization found that while the policy is officially termed "bilingual education," in practice it is "leading to the gradual replacement of Tibetan by Chinese as the medium of instruction in primary schools throughout the region" ([HRW](https://www.hrw.org/report/2020/03/05/chinas-bilingual-education-policy-tibet/tibetan-medium-schooling-under-threat)). The report documents that Chinese has been the language of instruction in nearly all middle and high schools in the TAR since the 1960s, and that new practices are extending Chinese-medium teaching into primary schools and kindergartens. HRW notes that non-Tibetan-speaking teachers in Tibetan schools tripled between 1988 and 2005, with 30,000 more planned to be sent to Tibet and Xinjiang by 2020.

The Tibetan Centre for Human Rights and Democracy (TCHRD) reported in 2023 that Chinese-medium education was being imposed in Ngaba Prefecture, "mandating the replacement of all Tibetan medium education systems with Chinese medium or Model 2," under which all subjects are taught in Chinese ([TCHRD](https://tchrd.org/2023/05/01/chinese-medium-education-imposed-in-schools-across-ngaba-despite-criticisms-from-tibetan-scholars-and-educators-2/)). The Save Tibet organization similarly reported that kindergarten and elementary schools are teaching in Chinese rather than Tibetan, "risking the children's" linguistic heritage ([Save Tibet](https://savetibet.org/chinese-replacing-tibetan-language-as-medium-of-instruction-in-tibet-report-says/)).

## Cultural Preservation Concerns

Beyond language, cultural preservation faces documented challenges. The U.S. State Department's 2023 human rights report states that "state policies disrupted traditional Tibetan culture" and notes restrictions on Tibetan cultural preservation discussions online ([State Department](https://2021-2025.state.gov/reports/2023-country-reports-on-human-rights-practices/china/tibet/)). The Congressional-Executive Commission on China held hearings in March 2023 on "cultural erasure" and "forced assimilation" in Tibet ([CECC](http://www.cecc.gov/events/hearings/preserving-tibet-combating-cultural-erasure-forced-assimilation-and-transnational)).

## Conclusion

While the Chinese government officially promotes bilingual education and cultural protection, substantial evidence from human rights organizations, academic researchers, and international bodies indicates that Tibetan-medium instruction is declining in practice, particularly in primary and pre-school education. The gap between official policy rhetoric and on-the-ground implementation remains a central point of contention in international assessments of language and cultural rights in Tibet.
