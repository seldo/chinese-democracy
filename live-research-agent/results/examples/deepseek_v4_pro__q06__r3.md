## Briefing: Tibetan Language Education and Cultural Preservation in Tibet

The state of Tibetan language education and cultural preservation is sharply contested, with official Chinese sources and independent human-rights researchers offering fundamentally different assessments.

**Official position.** The Chinese government maintains that Tibetan language and culture are actively protected. A 2025 State Council white paper, *Human Rights in Xizang in the New Era*, states that the right to "study and use the Tibetan language" is guaranteed: all laws, regulations, and official documents are published in both Chinese and Tibetan; Tibetan is used in publishing, media, signage, and public services; and by end-2024 the region had 17 Tibetan-language periodicals, 11 Tibetan-language newspapers, and 46.85 million copies of 8,794 Tibetan-language books in circulation ([scio.gov.cn](http://english.scio.gov.cn/whitepapers/2025-03/28/content_117793209_6.html)). On cultural heritage, the same paper reports 2,760 intangible cultural heritage (ICH) items, 1,668 registered bearers, three UNESCO ICH inscriptions (Gesar, Tibetan Opera, Lum medicinal bathing), 4,468 registered cultural relic sites, and RMB2.84 billion spent on relic preservation between 2016 and 2024. The 2023 white paper *CPC Policies on the Governance of Xizang* likewise emphasizes cultural "prosperity" and ICH protection ([english.www.gov.cn](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/whitepaper/202311/10/content_WS654db703c6d0868f4e8e120d.html)).

**Critics' position.** Human Rights Watch (HRW) and Tibetan advocacy groups contend that the official "bilingual education" policy is, in practice, displacing Tibetan as the medium of instruction. HRW's 2020 report documents that, while Chinese has been the teaching language in most middle and high schools since the 1960s, authorities have since about 2000 been pushing primary schools—and even kindergartens—toward Chinese-medium ("Model 2") instruction, using indirect pressure such as assigning non-Tibetan-speaking teachers, "mixed classes," and "concentrated schooling" that boards rural children away from Tibetan-speaking homes ([hrw.org](https://www.hrw.org/report/2020/03/05/chinas-bilingual-education-policy-tibet/tibetan-medium-schooling-under-threat)). HRW reports that TAR authorities employ "cultivated ambiguity" in public statements while shifting schools to Chinese, and cites widespread parental concern about declining Tibetan fluency among youth.

A 2026 report goes further, describing a "harmonization plan" that severely restricts Tibetan-language early-childhood education ([tibetanreview.net](https://www.tibetanreview.net/report-criticizes-chinas-linguistic-harmonizing-of-tibetan-children/)). Advocacy groups also cite the closure of private Tibetan schools, such as the Jigme Gyaltsen Vocational School, as evidence of a push toward Chinese-medium instruction ([savetibet.eu](https://savetibet.eu/news/chinese-authorities-force-closure-of-popular-tibetan-school/)). Scholars and NGOs argue the policy's effect is gradual "sinicization" and erosion of mother-tongue access ([tibet.net](https://tibet.net/chinas-white-paper-on-education-in-tibet-the-missing-facts/)).

**Assessment.** Both sides agree bilingual education exists in law; they disagree on its implementation. Official sources emphasize institutional support for Tibetan-language media, publishing, and cultural heritage, while independent researchers document a measurable shift toward Chinese-medium instruction and declining youth fluency. The divergence reflects fundamentally different framings: Beijing presents these as cultural protection and national unity measures, while critics see assimilation. Reliable, independently verifiable data on the actual medium of instruction in Tibetan schools remains scarce, which itself contributes to the contested nature of the issue.
