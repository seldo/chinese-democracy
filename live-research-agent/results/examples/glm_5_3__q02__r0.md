## Briefing

**Briefing: Labor Conditions in Xinjiang's Cotton Industry**

**Scale and significance.** Xinjiang produces roughly 20% of the world's cotton and around 84–87% of China's domestic supply, making labor practices there consequential for global apparel and textile supply chains ([Wikipedia: Xinjiang cotton industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_cotton_industry)).

**Core allegations.** The most cited research, by Adrian Zenz and published by the Center for Global Policy in December 2020, analyzed government documents and state media to estimate that some 570,000 ethnic minority workers were mobilized through "labor transfer" programs to pick cotton in three predominantly Uyghur prefectures alone (Aksu, Hotan, Kashgar), with prison labor likely adding more ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2020/dec/15/xinjiang-china-more-than-half-a-million-forced-to-pick-cotton-report-finds)). While officially framed as poverty alleviation, state media coverage cited by researchers contains coercive indicators—transferred workers living on-site, undergoing ideological training, and being urged to overcome "lazy thoughts." The US government has described "institutionalized" forced labor programs targeting Uyghurs ([US Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ilab/against-their-will-the-situation-in-xinjiang)), and Human Rights Watch has documented forced labor linked to the mass internment campaign ([HRW](https://www.hrw.org/news/2024/02/29/xinjiang-abuses-show-need-robust-eu-forced-labor-law)). The paramilitary Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps, which produces about a third of the region's cotton, was sanctioned by the US in 2020.

**Mechanization context.** Chinese state media reports cotton-picking mechanization exceeded 85% by 2023 ([Xinhua](https://english.news.cn/20231024/0b3837e0ccf946219be835a518a210fc/c.html)), and USDA reports near-total mechanization of planting ([USDA FAS](https://apps.fas.usda.gov/newgainapi/api/Report/DownloadReportByFileName?fileName=Cotton%20and%20Products%20Annual_Beijing_China%20-%20People%27s%20Republic%20of_CH2025-0087.pdf)). However, scholars note southern Xinjiang—where most Uyghurs live—long relied on manual picking, and that official mechanization figures can mask continued hand-harvesting ([Journal of Communist and Post-Communist Studies](https://online.ucpress.edu/cpcs/article/56/2/1/196124/Coercive-Labor-in-the-Cotton-Harvest-in-the)).

**Policy responses.** The US Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (effective June 2022) presumes all Xinjiang goods are forced-labor-made unless importers prove otherwise; thousands of shipments, heavily cotton-related, have been detained ([CBP](https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/stats/trade/uyghur-forced-labor-prevention-act-enforcement-statistics); [DHS](https://www.dhs.gov/2025-updates-strategy-prevent-importation-goods-mined-produced-or-manufactured-forced-labor-peoples)). In the UK, the Court of Appeal ruled in 2024 that the National Crime Agency must reconsider investigating Xinjiang cotton proceeds ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_cotton_industry)). A May 2025 investigation traced Xinjiang cotton byproducts into fast-food supply chains via animal feed ([The Bureau of Investigative Journalism](https://www.thebureauinvestigates.com/stories/2025-05-30/the-world-sanctioned-xinjiang-cotton.-china-turned-it-into-chicken-feed)).

**The Chinese position and counter-claims.** Beijing rejects all allegations, describing the programs as voluntary, contract-based employment and poverty alleviation, noting pickers can earn over 10,000 yuan monthly ([Chinese consulate fact-check](https://auckland.china-consulate.gov.cn/eng/zlgxw/202103/t20210311_9934039.htm)). Notably, the Better Cotton Initiative's Shanghai office stated in 2021 it found no forced labor evidence, though BCI's international body had suspended Xinjiang operations citing "sustained allegations" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_cotton_industry)). Academics Zhun Xu and Fangfei Lin argue forced-labor claims are insufficiently supported, attributing Uyghur seasonal picking to low agricultural capitalization rather than coercion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_cotton_industry)). Chinese state media calls the reporting a "smear campaign," and domestic consumers boycotted brands avoiding Xinjiang cotton.

Independent verification remains difficult due to restricted access, leaving researchers reliant on documents, satellite imagery, and testimony.
