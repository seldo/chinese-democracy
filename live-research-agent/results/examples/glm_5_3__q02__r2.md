## Briefing

**Labor Conditions in Xinjiang's Cotton Industry: A Briefing**

**Scale and context.** Xinjiang produces roughly 20% of the world's cotton and 85–90% of China's, with around 7 million rural residents—most from ethnic minority groups—engaged in cotton-related work ([Xinhua via Chinese Embassy](https://ca.china-embassy.gov.cn/eng/zt/xingjiang2021/202104/t20210401_9029489.htm); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_cotton_industry)).

**Forced labor allegations.** Research by Adrian Zenz for the Center for Global Policy, based on government documents and state media, estimated that at least 570,000 people from the predominantly Uyghur prefectures of Aksu, Hotan, and Kashgar were coerced into cotton picking through "labour transfer programs" in 2018 ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2020/dec/15/xinjiang-china-more-than-half-a-million-forced-to-pick-cotton-report-finds)). A BBC investigation documented official language describing pickers as "mobilised and organised" under quotas of "transferring all those who should be transferred," transported in groups, accompanied by officials who "eat, live, study and work with them," and subjected to "thought education." Documents also framed the goal as overcoming villagers' "deep-rooted, lazy thinking." One Uyghur exile reported his sister received no pay for three months of factory work, and that people comply "because they're afraid of being taken to jail" ([BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/extra/nz0g306v8c/china-tainted-cotton)). Researchers note that under international conventions, paid labor can still constitute forced labor; reported wages varied widely, from claims of 5,000 RMB/month to one village averaging 1,670 RMB ([BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/extra/nz0g306v8c/china-tainted-cotton)). Scholars also draw parallels with Uzbekistan's state-organized cotton forced labor ([UC Press, Critical Sociology](https://online.ucpress.edu/cpcs/article/56/2/1/196124/Coercive-Labor-in-the-Cotton-Harvest-in-the)).

**Mechanization.** China reports the mechanized harvest rate reached nearly 70% in 2020 and over 85% by 2023, arguing this makes mass forced labor implausible ([Xinhua](https://ca.china-embassy.gov.cn/eng/zt/xingjiang2021/202104/t20210401_9029489.htm)). However, analysts note southern Xinjiang—where most cotton is grown and Uyghurs are concentrated—remains far more reliant on manual picking than the mechanized XPCC (paramilitary corps) farms ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2020/dec/15/xinjiang-china-more-than-half-a-million-forced-to-pick-cotton-report-finds)).

**Chinese government position and counterarguments.** Beijing calls the allegations "completely fabricated," stating workers choose jobs freely, sign voluntary contracts, and that poverty fell from ~20% to ~1% ([BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/extra/nz0g306v8c/china-tainted-cotton)). Academics Zhun Xu and Fangfei Lin argue there is insufficient evidence of forced labor, citing the long history of Uyghur seasonal pickers and low agricultural capitalization in the south ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_cotton_industry)).

**International response and verification limits.** The Better Cotton Initiative suspended and then ceased Xinjiang operations in 2020 citing "sustained allegations," though its Shanghai office later said it found no evidence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_cotton_industry)). The US banned XPCC cotton in 2020 and all Xinjiang cotton in 2021; a UK court ordered the National Crime Agency to reconsider an investigation in 2024. Independent verification remains extremely difficult: journalists and auditors report being tailed, blocked, and denied access ([BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/extra/nz0g306v8c/china-tainted-cotton)).
