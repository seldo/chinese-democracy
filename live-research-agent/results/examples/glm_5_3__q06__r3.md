## Briefing

**Tibetan Language Education and Cultural Preservation: A Contested Landscape**

The state of Tibetan language education and cultural preservation in Tibet is deeply contested, with sharply divergent accounts from the Chinese government versus human rights organizations, UN experts, and exile groups. Independent verification is difficult because foreign researchers and journalists face severe access restrictions in Tibetan areas ([HRW](https://www.hrw.org/report/2026/05/04/start-with-youngest-children/chinas-use-of-preschools-to-integrate-tibetans)).

**Language education policy.** China's laws nominally guarantee minority language rights. The 1984 Regional Nationality Autonomy Law required schools in autonomous areas to use minority languages as the medium of instruction "whenever possible." However, Human Rights Watch documents that a July 2021 Ministry of Education "Children's Speech Harmonization Plan" mandated Mandarin as the language of instruction and care in all preschools, including in Tibetan areas, effectively ending even nominal bilingual commitments. Recent visitors report children as young as three or four rapidly losing Tibetan fluency, with consequences for intergenerational transmission of culture and religion ([HRW](https://www.hrw.org/report/2026/05/04/start-with-youngest-children/chinas-use-of-preschools-to-integrate-tibetans)). In February 2023, UN human rights experts raised alarm over an estimated one million Tibetan children separated from their families into state-run boarding and preschool systems taught primarily in Mandarin ([UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2023/02/1133212)). Advocacy groups report similar Mandarin-first shifts in Tibetan areas of Sichuan and Qinghai ([International Campaign for Tibet](https://savetibet.org/ngaba-highlights-erosion-of-tibetan-language-instruction/)).

**The government's position.** Beijing rejects assimilation claims, insisting its policies promote "ethnic integration" while respecting minority cultures. Official white papers cite over 30,000 bilingual teachers, some 5,800 Tibetan-language teachers, and 821 Tibetan-language textbooks compiled for regional schools ([Chinese government white paper](http://english.www.gov.cn/archive/white_paper/2015/09/06/content_281475183815861.htm)). The 2023 white paper on Tibet governance highlights state protection of Tibetan classics and intangible cultural heritage ([State Council](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/whitepaper/202311/10/content_WS654db703c6d0868f4e8e120d.html)). China reports spending roughly 300 million yuan on intangible cultural heritage protection in Tibet over 13 years, and extensive restoration of the UNESCO-listed Potala Palace, including digitization of thousands of ancient manuscripts ([China Daily](http://epaper.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202409/24/WS66f1ee39a310b6be0c6575dd.html)).

**Cultural preservation concerns.** Critics argue heritage spending masks deeper erosion. The U.S. Congressional-Executive Commission on China has heard testimony on "cultural erasure and forced assimilation," including restrictions on religious practice and the exclusion of Tibetan Buddhist content from curricula ([CECC](http://www.cecc.gov/events/hearings/preserving-tibet-combating-cultural-erasure-forced-assimilation-and-transnational)). UNESCO-designated sites like the Potala are criticized as "musealized" heritage divorced from living practice ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/707/)). The UN Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights has questioned China's assimilation policies ([ICESCR review](https://savetibet.org/un-economic-social-and-cultural-rights-committee-addresses-chinas-assimilation-of-tibetans/)).

**Assessment.** While China has invested substantially in physical heritage preservation and literacy, the balance of evidence indicates that Tibetan's role as a living medium of instruction has declined sharply since 2021, and religious-cultural expression faces tightening constraints—trends advocates warn could endanger the language within a generation.
