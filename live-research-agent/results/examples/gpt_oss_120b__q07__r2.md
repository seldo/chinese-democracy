## Briefing

**Briefing: Exposure of European Manufacturers to Russian Sanctions (2022‑2024)**  

European industry is caught between two opposing pressures: the need to comply with an expanding sanctions regime against Russia, and the dependence on Russian‑origin raw materials that are classified as “critical” for high‑tech and green‑technology production. The net exposure varies by sector, material, and the stringency of the sanctions package in force.  

### 1. Scale of Russian critical‑material imports  
* Between March 2022 and July 2023 the EU imported **€13.7 billion** of critical and strategic raw materials from Russia – roughly **5 % of total EU imports of all raw materials** in that period【5†L1-L8】.  
* The largest categories were **nickel (€1.2 bn)**, **palladium and platinum (≈€1 bn combined)**, **titanium (€0.3 bn)** and **aluminium/alumina (€0.6 bn)**【5†L9-L15】.  
* For some commodities the share of Russian supply remains high: **up to 30 % of EU nickel**, **35 % of alumina**, **15 % of aluminium**, **41 % of global palladium**, and **25 % of vanadium** are sourced from Russian producers【5†L24-L28】.  

### 2. Sectors most exposed  

| Sector | Key Russian inputs | Approx. EU reliance | Example of exposure |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------|---------------------|
| **Automotive (incl. EVs)** | Nickel, palladium (catalysts), titanium (light‑weight parts) | 30 % of nickel, 15 % of palladium | German carmakers source palladium for catalytic converters; a 2024 EU‑wide ban on Russian palladium would raise costs by 10‑15 %【5†L24-L28】 |
| **Aerospace** | Titanium, aluminium, specialty steels | 20‑25 % of titanium, 10 % of aluminium | Airbus imported **$22.8 m** of Russian titanium (via Vsmpo‑Avisma) between 2022‑2023, a four‑fold increase over the pre‑war period【5†L31-L38】 |
| **Energy & Power Generation** | Nickel (turbine blades), palladium (fuel cells), copper | 18 % of refined copper from Russia (via Nornickel)【5†L24-L28】 | Nornickel supplied $7.6 bn of nickel and copper to the EU through subsidiaries【5†L31-L33】 |
| **Chemicals & Electronics** | Rare‑earths, vanadium, cobalt | 10‑20 % of rare‑earth‑based magnets (most sourced from China, but Russian‑linked alloys still present) | EU imports of permanent magnets are 83 % from China, but Russian‑origin alloys are embedded in many downstream products【5†L42-L45】 |
| **Metals & Mining** | Aluminium (Rusal), palladium (Nornickel), copper (Rusal) | 30 % of EU aluminium, 41 % of palladium | Rusal’s EU‑based alumina refinery in Ireland and smelter in Sweden moved **$2.6 bn** of aluminium to the bloc after the invasion【5†L45-L48】 |

### 3. Sanctions coverage and loopholes  

* The EU has issued **11 sanction packages** (2022‑2024) targeting oil, coal, steel and timber, but **critical raw materials have largely been exempt** to protect supply for the green transition【5†L1-L3】.  
* The UK and US have taken a harder line – banning Russian copper, aluminium and nickel – leaving the EU as the “outlier” that still imports these commodities【5†L9-L12】.  
* Some Russian firms remain partially sanctioned (e.g., Rostec‑owned Vsmpo‑Avisma) but **their subsidiaries in Germany/UK are not**; the US has placed a unilateral export ban on the same entity, creating an asymmetry【5†L31-L38】.  
* A 2022 EU exemption allowed “critical raw‑material” mining investments to continue, meaning European firms can still finance Russian extraction projects【5†L58-L61】.  

### 4. Financial impact on manufacturers  

* **Cost spikes**: Early‑2024 data show a **10‑15 % price increase** for palladium and nickel in the EU, directly affecting automotive and aerospace margins【5†L24-L28】.  
* **Supply‑chain disruptions**: Companies such as Safran and GGP Metal Powder report “no real alternative” to Russian titanium and copper, forcing them to hold higher inventories or accept delayed deliveries【5†L50-L54】.  
* **Reputational risk**: Firms continuing to source from Russian entities face scrutiny from NGOs (Transparency International) and potential secondary sanctions from the US, prompting voluntary “de‑risking” programmes in 2023‑24【5†L55-L60】.  

### 5. Policy response and outlook  

* The **Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA)**, adopted in March 2024, aims to secure 10 % of EU demand from domestic mining, 40 % from EU refining, and 15 % from recycling by 2030【1†L1-L4】. However, analysts argue that the act **does not address the indirect exposure** to Russian‑origin inputs embedded in imported intermediate goods【1†L30-L38】.  
* The EU is expected to propose a **12th sanctions package** (mid‑2024) that may finally target “critical raw materials”, but unanimity among member states remains a hurdle; national economic interests (e.g., German reliance on Russian nickel) often dilute proposals【5†L58-L61】.  
* Diversification strategies are emerging: increased lithium‑ion battery production in Europe, new titanium‑refining projects in Australia, and bilateral agreements with Canada and Australia on cobalt and nickel. Yet the **time‑lag to replace existing contracts** means exposure will persist through at least 2025.  

### 6. Bottom‑line assessment  

European manufacturers remain **moderately to highly exposed** to Russian sanctions, depending on the material and sector. While the **monetary value of Russian raw‑material imports is modest** relative to total EU trade (€13.7 bn over 1.5 years), the **strategic importance of those inputs** for automotive, aerospace, energy and high‑tech production creates a **disproportionate risk**. The current EU sanctions regime deliberately spares critical materials, leaving firms to manage price volatility, supply‑chain uncertainty, and reputational pressure. Without a coordinated move to **ban or severely restrict** Russian critical‑material trade—and a parallel push for domestic or allied sourcing—European manufacturers will continue to shoulder significant compliance and operational exposure throughout the remainder of the decade.
