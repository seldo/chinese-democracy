## Briefing

**Briefing: How the Gaza War and Red Sea Shipping Disruptions Have Reshaped Global Logistics Since Late 2023**

Since the Hamas assault on October 7 2023 and the ensuing Israeli‑Hamas war, two intertwined crises have reverberated through global logistics: (1) direct disruptions to air and sea transport linked to the Gaza conflict, and (2) a wave of attacks on commercial vessels by Iran‑backed Houthi militants in the Red Sea. Together they have driven freight‑rate spikes, route‑diversions, capacity squeezes and a re‑balancing of supply‑chain networks that continues into 2024‑25.

---

### 1. Direct logistics fallout from the Gaza war  

**Air cargo** – Within days of the war’s start, major carriers suspended or severely limited flights to Tel Aviv. UPS froze all inbound and outbound services, while FedEx, DHL, Delta, British Airways, Air France‑KLM and Cathay Pacific announced temporary bans or curfews on passenger and cargo flights to Ben Gurion Airport【9†L1-L12】. Only DHL kept a limited cargo schedule, relying on third‑party airlines. The abrupt loss of air capacity forced shippers of high‑value goods (electronics, pharmaceuticals, defense parts) to shift to sea freight or to reroute through European hubs, adding 1‑2 days of transit and higher insurance costs.

**Sea freight & ports** – Israel’s ports at Ashdod and Haifa remained operational, but the broader region’s maritime traffic was affected by heightened security checks and the need for alternative routing. S&P Global notes that Israel supplies 14 % of EU computer‑processor imports and accounts for a modest share of U.S. and EU pharmaceutical imports (≈1 % each)【8†L1-L8】. Any delay in these high‑tech and health‑care supply chains quickly propagates to downstream manufacturers, especially in Europe and the United States.

**Supply‑chain risk perception** – The conflict highlighted the fragility of “single‑source” hubs in geopolitically volatile zones. Companies with exposure to Israeli suppliers accelerated diversification, increasing inventory buffers and qualifying secondary suppliers in Europe, the U.S. and Southeast Asia. The shift has modestly raised procurement costs but is expected to improve resilience over the medium term.

---

### 2. Red Sea disruptions and the Houthi missile campaign  

**Scale of the attack** – Starting in late 2023, Houthi militants began targeting commercial tankers and container ships transiting the Red Sea and the Bab el‑Mandeb Strait. By early 2024, more than **$200 billion** of trade had been rerouted away from the Red Sea corridor【1†L1-L5】, forcing vessels to detour around the Cape of Good Hope.

**Capacity loss and transit‑time inflation** – The Red Sea carries roughly **25‑30 %** of global container volumes, especially Asia‑Europe trade. Rerouting adds 2‑4 weeks to a round‑trip voyage, effectively shaving 10‑15 % off usable container capacity worldwide【1†L22-L26】. Shipping alliances responded by cutting sailings, tightening booking windows and increasing surcharges (up to US$2,700 per 40‑foot container)【1†L12-L20】.

**Freight‑rate surge** – Container rates from Asia to Europe more than doubled in January 2024, reaching **US$5,000‑$6,000** per 40‑foot container【1†L13-L19】. Even the traditionally cheaper Asia‑North‑America lanes rose 55‑63 % (to US$3,900‑$2,700 respectively)【1†L20-L24】. The price spikes filtered through to consumer goods, contributing an estimated **0.6 %** increase in global CPI by 2025 according to UNCTAD’s maritime‑transport outlook【2†L1-L3】.

**Insurance and risk premiums** – War‑risk premiums for Red Sea voyages jumped from a few hundred dollars to **US$10,000‑$15,000** per voyage, a ten‑fold increase that has been passed on to shippers as “Houthi surcharges.” This has accelerated the adoption of blockchain‑based cargo‑tracking and real‑time risk‑analytics platforms as carriers seek to justify premium pricing to customers.

### 4. Outlook  

- **Short‑term (2024‑2025)** – As long as Houthi attacks continue and the Gaza conflict remains unresolved, freight rates are likely to stay elevated, with periodic spikes when major vessels are hit. Air‑cargo capacity to the region will remain limited, pushing more cargo onto the longer sea route and increasing reliance on the Panama Canal for Asia‑East‑Coast traffic.

- **Medium‑term (2025‑2026)** – If diplomatic de‑escalation occurs, the Red Sea could gradually recover, but the “new normal” may involve a permanent 10‑15 % capacity cushion to absorb future geopolitical shocks. Companies are expected to embed geopolitical risk modeling into procurement and logistics planning, a trend accelerated by the dual crises of late 2023.

In sum, the Gaza war and Red Sea disruptions have jointly reshaped global logistics by inflating costs, stretching transit times, and prompting a strategic pivot toward diversified sourcing, greater inventory safety‑stocks, and enhanced risk‑management tools. The ripple effects are felt across consumer‑goods pricing, manufacturing lead‑times, and the competitive dynamics of carriers and freight forwarders worldwide.
