# Briefing: Tibetan Language Education and Cultural Preservation in Tibet

The state of Tibetan language education is sharply contested, with the Chinese government and independent observers offering near-opposite assessments.

**The official Chinese position.** Beijing maintains that Tibetan language and culture are protected and flourishing. Its November 2023 white paper, "CPC Policies on the Governance of Xizang in the New Era," cites achievements including standardized Tibetan terminology (2,200 new approved terms), cultural heritage protection, and state support for Tibetan opera, festivals, and tourism as vehicles of cultural transmission (https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/whitepaper/202311/10/content_WS654db703c6d0868f4e8e120d.html). Earlier white papers similarly emphasized Tibetan-language publishing, bilingual schooling, and intangible heritage listings (http://www.china.org.cn/government/whitepaper/node_7054682.htm).

**Criticisms from rights groups and UN experts.** Human Rights Watch reports that a 2021 Ministry of Education directive—the "Children's Speech Harmonization" plan—mandates Mandarin for all preschool instruction in minority areas, ending Tibetan-medium kindergartens, the last educational level where Tibetan was a primary language of instruction. Interviewees reported children switching almost entirely to Chinese within months of starting kindergarten (https://www.hrw.org/news/2026/05/04/china-harmonization-plan-erasing-tibetan-language). A 2020 HRW report found Chinese replacing Tibetan as the medium of instruction in primary schools (https://savetibet.org/chinese-replacing-tibetan-language-as-medium-of-instruction-in-tibet-report-says/).

In February 2023, UN special rapporteurs expressed alarm that roughly one million Tibetan children attend state-run boarding schools where curricula are almost entirely in Mandarin, warning of forced assimilation and family separation (https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2023/02/china-un-experts-alarmed-separation-1-million-tibetan-children-families-and). Beijing rejects this, saying boarding attendance is voluntary and necessary in remote areas.

**Additional pressure points.** The Tibet Policy Institute documents school closures (including monastic and vocational schools teaching in Tibetan), a 2025 ban on private Tibetan-language tutoring during winter break, removal of Tibetan from the Douyin app, and prosecution of language advocates such as Tashi Wangchuk, sentenced to five years in prison in 2018 (https://tibetpolicy.net/chinas-language-policy-in-tibet/). Critics note the 2026 Law on Promoting Ethnic Unity and Progress penalizes "obstructing" the learning of Chinese, and that "bilingual education" now effectively means Chinese-medium instruction with Tibetan as a subject at best.

**Assessment.** There is broad factual agreement that Mandarin-medium instruction now dominates Tibetan-area schooling from kindergarten onward, and that the state invests in Tibetan cultural heritage (opera, festivals, manuscripts, terminology standardization). The dispute is over interpretation: Beijing frames this as modernization plus preservation; critics, including UN experts, argue that heritage display coexists with policies eroding Tibetan as a living language of education and daily use. Restricted independent access to Tibetan areas makes verification difficult, but the trajectory—documented in China's own policy documents—points to a systematic shift from Tibetan-medium to Chinese-medium education over the past two decades, with significant implications for intergenerational language transmission.
