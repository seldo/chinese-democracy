## Briefing

Hong Kong’s status has **rebounded in headline rankings but changed in character**: it is again rated near the very top, yet it is more dependent on mainland Chinese liquidity and policy support, and operates under tighter political/legal constraints than before 2020.

At the start of the period, the city was already slipping: GFCI 27, published 26 March 2020, said Hong Kong fell from third to sixth after the 2019 unrest (https://www.longfinance.net/programmes/distributed-futures/publications/global-financial-centres-index-27/). Beijing then imposed the National Security Law in mid-2020; Reuters later assessed that the law “reshaped Hong Kong’s political landscape” (https://www.reuters.com/graphics/HONGKONG-SECURITY/PROSECUTIONS/lbpgyjlqbpq/). Washington moved to end Hong Kong’s separate treatment under U.S. law—significant because that special status underpinned confidence—though Hong Kong’s finance chief argued the direct sanctions impact would be limited (https://www.brookings.edu/articles/whats-at-stake-in-chinas-new-national-security-legislation-for-hong-kong/; https://news.cgtn.com/news/2020-05-31/U-S-sanctions-on-HKSAR-will-be-limited-finance-chief-QWMT280rII/index.html).

The clearest reputational recovery is in the Z/Yen index: Hong Kong ranked third globally in GFCI 38 with a rating of 764 (https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202509/25/P2025092500970.htm) and maintained third, first in Asia-Pacific, at 765 in March 2026 (https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202603/26/P2026032600501.htm). Market activity tells a similar bust-and-recovery story. After the zero-COVID/China slowdown years, Hong Kong’s IPO market only recorded its first rise in new listings since 2020 in 2024, raising about US$10.65bn across 63 deals (https://www.cnbc.com/2024/12/18/hong-kong-records-first-rise-in-new-listings-since-2020-as-beijing-policy-fuels-optimism.html). In 2025 it then became the world’s top IPO fundraising venue, with proceeds up 231% year-on-year to US$37.4bn (https://www.hkexgroup.com/Media-Centre/Insight/Insight/2026/HKEX-Insight/ECM-performance-in-2025?sc_lang=en). Turnover also surged: average daily turnover reached HK$249.8bn in 2025, up 90% from HK$131.8bn in 2024 (https://www.hkex.com.hk/Market-Data/Statistics/Consolidated-Reports/HKEX-Monthly-Market-Highlights?sc_lang=en).

But the driver is narrower and more mainland-linked. Southbound Stock Connect average daily turnover rose from HK$0.9bn in 2014 to HK$121.1bn in 2025 (https://www.hkexgroup.com/Media-Centre/Insight/Insight/2026/HKEX-Insight/Stock-Connect-2025-Review?sc_lang=en), showing Hong Kong’s growing role as China’s offshore capital-raising and trading hub rather than a purely global entrepôt. The talent picture is mixed: in early 2022 Reuters reported a financial-sector talent crunch as expatriates left, with sector applicants down 23% (https://www.reuters.com/markets/europe/hong-kongs-financial-sector-faces-talent-crunch-expats-head-exit-2022-01-23/). The government responded with admission schemes including the Top Talent Pass Scheme (https://www.immd.gov.hk/eng/useful_information/admission-schemes-talents-professionals-entrepreneurs.html).

Net assessment: Hong Kong remains a top-tier international financial centre—still advantaged by convertibility, deep markets and China access—but its “international” standing is more conditional on geopolitics, mainland confidence and perceptions of legal autonomy than it was in 2020.
