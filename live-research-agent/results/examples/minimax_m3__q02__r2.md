# Labor Conditions in Xinjiang's Cotton Industry: A Briefing

Xinjiang produces roughly 20% of the world's cotton and around 84–87% of China's domestic supply, making it the single most important cotton-producing region globally ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_cotton_industry); [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2020/dec/15/xinjiang-china-more-than-half-a-million-forced-to-pick-cotton-report-finds)). The industry employs hundreds of thousands of workers and underpins the livelihoods of more than half of Xinjiang's farmers ([Xinhua](https://english.news.cn/20240607/001becc3cf6444e3905fd30f0c07d6e0/c.html)).

## Allegations of Forced Labor

Multiple investigations have documented coercive labor practices in cotton production. A December 2020 report by researcher Adrian Zenz, based on Chinese government documents and state media, estimated that more than 500,000 minority laborers were mobilized to pick cotton in southern Xinjiang through state-run "labor transfer" programs ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2020/dec/15/xinjiang-china-more-than-half-a-million-forced-to-pick-cotton-report-finds)). A 2023 peer-reviewed study published in *Comparative Political Studies* found that these coercive transfers continued through at least 2022 and remain embedded in Xinjiang's official Five-Year Plan ([Foreign Policy](https://foreignpolicy.com/2023/05/16/china-xinjiang-uyghurs-cotton-forced-labor/)). The U.S. State Department's 2024 Human Rights Report concluded that Xinjiang's cotton output is "systematically tainted" by state-imposed labor programs targeting Uyghurs and other minorities ([Ecotextile News](https://www.ecotextile.com/2025081859146/news/social-compliance-and-csr/report-flags-ongoing-forced-labour-risks-in-chinas-cotton-industry/)).

Mechanization has not eliminated these concerns. While mechanized harvesting covers about 83% of Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps (XPCC) farms, southern Xinjiang's premium long-staple cotton still requires manual picking, and land consolidation for mechanization has itself displaced farmers into state-assigned factory work ([Foreign Policy](https://foreignpolicy.com/2023/05/16/china-xinjiang-uyghurs-cotton-forced-labor/)).

## Chinese Government Response

Beijing rejects the allegations. State media and officials characterize labor transfers as voluntary poverty-alleviation and vocational-training programs, and have accused Western critics of fabricating claims to harm China's economy ([CGTN](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2021-03-25/China-slams-fabricated-forced-labor-lies-on-China-s-Xinjiang-YV41EGrbZ6/index.html)). Xinhua reports that cotton-related sanctions have caused "forced unemployment," shuttering factories and textile enterprises in southern Xinjiang ([Xinhua](https://english.news.cn/20240607/001becc3cf6444e3905fd30f0c07d6e0/c.html)). Some Western-based researchers, including economists Zhun Xu and Fangfei Lin, have argued that Uyghur participation in cotton harvesting reflects long-standing labor patterns rather than ethnic targeting ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_cotton_industry)).

## International Policy Response

The United States imposed import restrictions on XPCC cotton in September 2020, banned all Xinjiang cotton in January 2021, and enacted the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act in December 2021, which presumes forced labor in any Xinjiang-origin good unless proven otherwise. The EU is debating comparable legislation. The Better Cotton Initiative suspended field activities in Xinjiang in 2020, though its Shanghai office later stated it found no evidence of forced labor ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_cotton_industry)).

The dispute remains unresolved. Reports from NGOs, academics, and Western governments point to documented coercive recruitment, while Chinese sources and a minority of outside scholars emphasize voluntary employment and economic harms caused by sanctions.
