# Briefing: The State of Tibetan Language Education and Cultural Preservation in Tibet

Tibetan language education and cultural preservation in Tibet is a deeply contested issue, with sharply divergent narratives from Chinese authorities and international human rights organizations.

## The Chinese Government's Position

Beijing maintains that it actively protects and develops Tibetan culture. According to a Chinese government white paper, the Tibet Autonomous Region (TAR) has legal frameworks guaranteeing Tibetan language use, including the 2002 Provisions on the Learning, Use and Development of Tibetan, which stipulate that "equal importance is given to both Tibetan and Chinese in Tibet, with priority given to Tibetan" (https://ie.china-embassy.gov.cn/eng/zt/ChinasTibet/201404/t20140410_2540532.htm). The white paper claims there are 15,523 bilingual teachers and 10,927 Tibetan-language teachers in Tibet, with 181 textbooks compiled for primary through senior high school. State media also point to Tibetan-language broadcasting, publishing houses, and an international-standard Tibetan character code approved by ISO in 1997.

## Concerns from International Organizations

Human Rights Watch research tells a different story. A 2020 report found that Mandarin has become the medium of instruction in approximately 95% of schools in Tibetan areas, including kindergartens, with Tibetan reduced to a single subject class rather than a teaching medium (https://www.hrw.org/news/2020/04/15/tibet-its-crime-even-talk-about-value-mother-tongue-education). HRW documented that schools are increasingly staffed by non-Tibetan-speaking teachers with virtually no Tibetan textbooks, and that advocating for mother-tongue education has been criminalized. In 2018, regional police in China declared organizations campaigning on "mother tongue" issues illegal as a form of "underworld gang crime."

In February 2023, UN human rights experts expressed alarm that approximately one million Tibetan children were being separated from their families through government boarding school policies (https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2023/02/china-un-experts-alarmed-separation-1-million-tibetan-children-families-and). The Tibet Action Institute estimates that three out of four Tibetan students are now in boarding schools, with children as young as four removed from their linguistic and cultural environments (https://tibetaction.net/colonialboardingschools/).

## Local Resistance and Recent Developments

Tibetans have resisted these policies through petitions, social media posts, and demonstrations. In 2020, residents of Ngaba (Aba) Prefecture in Sichuan opposed announced plans to phase out Tibetan-medium instruction in primary and middle schools, with intellectuals arguing that "mother tongue education is the best tool for learning and imparting knowledge" (https://savetibet.org/ngaba-highlights-erosion-of-tibetan-language-instruction/). The International Campaign for Tibet documented how authorities censored online discussions and ordered Tibetan CPPCC members not to raise the issue publicly.

## Assessment

The contradiction between official policy and on-the-ground practice is striking. While Chinese law formally protects minority language rights, the practical trajectory since the 2010 bilingual education reforms—and accelerating under Xi Jinping's emphasis on "ethnic mingling" and political identification—has shifted instruction decisively toward Mandarin. Whether this constitutes "cultural preservation" or "cultural erosion" depends largely on whether one evaluates legal frameworks or implementation outcomes, and whether cultural vitality is measured by institutional infrastructure or by intergenerational transmission within families and communities.
