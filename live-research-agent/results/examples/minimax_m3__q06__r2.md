# Briefing: State of Tibetan Language Education and Cultural Preservation in Tibet

The state of Tibetan language education and cultural preservation in Tibet is a deeply contested subject, with sharply divergent assessments from the Chinese government, United Nations human rights bodies, Tibetan advocacy organizations, and academic researchers.

## The Chinese Government's Position

Beijing maintains that Tibetan culture has been protected and developed since 1951, citing legal frameworks including the Constitution, the Law on Ethnic Regional Autonomy, and the 2002 Provisions of the Tibet Autonomous Region on the Learning, Use and Development of Tibetan, which give "equal importance to both Tibetan and Chinese in Tibet, with priority given to Tibetan." According to a 2008 State Council white paper, there are over 15,000 bilingual teachers and nearly 11,000 Tibetan-language teachers in Tibet, with 181 textbooks translated across 16 subjects from primary to senior high school (https://newyork.china-consulate.gov.cn/eng/xw/200809/t20080926_4688294.htm). The government also points to Tibetan-language media, including a satellite TV channel broadcasting 24 hours daily since 2007, and over 1,000 Tibetan-language book titles published annually. China frames its boarding schools as an anti-poverty and educational-access measure suited to the sparsely populated plateau.

## UN and Advocacy Concerns

These claims have been challenged by UN human rights mechanisms. On 6 February 2023, three UN Special Rapporteurs issued a joint public statement expressing "alarm" at a residential schooling system affecting around one million Tibetan children, calling on China to abolish it (https://tibetanevents.com/understand-tibet/boarding-schools-in-tibet/). On 5 February 2026, the Special Rapporteur on minority issues concluded that China's policies toward Tibetan language and identity "meet the threshold of concern for genocide as defined in international law." The Tibet Action Institute, drawing on Chinese state data, found that by 2019 roughly 800,000 to 900,000 Tibetan children aged six to eighteen were in boarding schools—approximately four out of five children in that age group—and that at least 100,000 children were in mandatory boarding preschools from age four. Critics argue that instruction is "overwhelmingly in Chinese," with Tibetan reduced to "one subject among others rather than the language of learning," and that family separation breaks intergenerational transmission of language and customs.

## Shifting Educational Policy

The trajectory has moved from genuine bilingual education toward Chinese-medium instruction. In 2010, proposed reforms in Qinghai that would have ended Tibetan as a medium of instruction triggered student protests; four UN Special Rapporteurs issued a joint urgent appeal, warning that restrictions "would have a negative impact on those of Tibetan origin and the preservation and promotion of the Tibetan language generally" (https://savetibet.org/tibetan-language-un-human-rights-experts-urgent-intervention-with-china/). A 2021 Ministry of Education directive—the "Children's Speech Harmonization" plan—mandated standard Mandarin for preschool instruction (https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/2140082.html), and the 2026 Ethnic Unity and Progress Law embeds the goal of a "unified national consciousness and common national language" in national law.

## Cultural Preservation Beyond Schools

Tibetan cultural preservation extends beyond formal education. The Chinese government points to the protection of historical sites, monastic institutions, and intangible heritage, though critics have raised concerns about commercialization and the political management of monasteries. In exile, institutions such as the Tibetan Children's Villages and the Library of Tibetan Works and Archives in Dharamsala have sustained language and religious education, with researchers documenting both the vibrancy of exile culture and the urgency of transmission disrupted inside Tibet (https://www.tibetchild.org/preserving-a-culture-in-exile/).

The fundamental disagreement concerns not the existence of Tibetan-language programs but whether current policies strengthen or erode Tibetan as a living medium of instruction and family transmission.
