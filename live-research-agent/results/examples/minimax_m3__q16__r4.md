## Briefing

# Hong Kong's Status as an International Financial Centre Since 2020

Hong Kong's standing as a global financial hub has faced its most significant test in decades since the imposition of the National Security Law (NSL) by Beijing in June 2020. The trajectory since then has been marked by both structural challenges and determined policy responses.

**Competitive Position Remains Strong on Paper**

Despite political headwinds, Hong Kong has retained its position among the world's top financial centres. In the Global Financial Centres Index (GFCI) 37, released in March 2025, Hong Kong ranked third globally with a rating of 760, trailing only New York and London and ahead of Singapore (750) ([Long Finance GFCI 37](https://www.longfinance.net/programmes/financial-centre-futures/global-financial-centres-index/gfci-37-explore-the-data/gfci-37-rank/); [HK Government Press Release](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202503/20/P2025032000391.htm)). Hong Kong ranked first globally in investment management, insurance, and finance, and second in human capital and infrastructure. Its fintech ranking climbed five places to fourth worldwide.

**Significant Headwinds**

However, underlying indicators tell a more mixed story. Hong Kong's IPO market contracted sharply: proceeds plunged roughly 60% in 2023 to HK$41.3 billion, with first-quarter 2024 fundraising down another 29% year-on-year ([Hong Kong Business](https://hongkongbusiness.hk/markets-investing/exclusive/mainlands-clampdown-ipos-likely-boost-listings-hong-kong); [CBIL](https://cbil.com.hk/news-and-insights/114)). The Hang Seng Index fell to multi-year lows, the property market dropped approximately 30%, and India's stock market overtook Hong Kong as the world's fourth-largest by capitalisation ([Freiheit China Bulletin](https://www.freiheit.org/hong-kong-become-former-financial-hub)). More than 600,000 residents emigrated between 2020 and 2023, depleting the professional middle class that underpinned the financial services sector.

**Capital Reallocation to Singapore**

Wealthy mainland Chinese and Hong Kong-based investors increasingly redirected assets to Singapore, which overtook Hong Kong in several regional financial inflow rankings. Singapore captured a disproportionate share of new family office formations, with single-family-office structures quadrupling in both cities since 2020 ([Copia Wealth Studios](https://copiawealthstudios.com/blog/inside-the-new-family-office-structure-building-sovereign-systems)).

**Policy Response and Resilience**

Hong Kong authorities responded with a multi-pronged reform agenda. The 2022 Policy Address set a target of attracting 200 family offices by 2025, supported by tax concessions and a new licensing regime ([InvestHK Tracker](https://ctacquisitions.com/guides/singapore-hong-kong-dubai-sfo-boom-tracker-2024-2026/)). Stock exchange reforms lowered listing thresholds for specialist technology companies in August 2024, while Bond Connect and Wealth Management Connect schemes were expanded to deepen mainland integration ([HK Government](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202503/20/P2025032000391.htm)). Assets under management grew 2% to over HK$31 trillion by end-2023 ([SCIO](http://english.scio.gov.cn/chinavoices/2024-09/26/content_117450795.html)). Notably, by late 2025 some Chinese family offices began relocating back from Singapore to Hong Kong amid tightening KYC requirements abroad ([Cryptopolitan](https://www.cryptopolitan.com/the-rich-of-china-are-exiting-singapore/)).

**Conclusion**

Hong Kong's financial centre status has not collapsed but has narrowed and reoriented. It retains elite rankings in capital markets and RMB infrastructure, yet faces persistent challenges from demographic decline, geopolitical tension, and intensified Singapore competition. Its trajectory now depends heavily on its ability to serve as the primary gateway for mainland capital while reassuring international investors concerned about the post-2020 legal environment.
