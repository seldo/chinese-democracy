**Assessing European Manufacturers’ Exposure to Russia Sanctions**

Since February 2022, the European Union has imposed 17 packages of restrictive measures targeting Russia’s finance, energy, defense, and dual-use export sectors. While the overarching goal has been to degrade Russia’s war economy, the impact on European manufacturers is highly uneven. Large original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) in the automotive and machinery sectors often report limited direct sales exposure to Russia, but significant indirect exposure persists through global supply chains, dual-use components, and chemical intermediates that are increasingly routed via third‑country intermediaries.

A Kyiv Independent investigation published in February 2026 highlights one of the most persistent sanction gaps: the continued flow of European‑sourced industrial chemicals—particularly raw glycerin and motor‑oil additives—into Russia via EU‑linked logistics firms. Glycerin, which can be converted into nitroglycerin for propellants and explosives, remains largely unrestricted despite its military applications. The investigation traced shipments through companies such as Delfin Group and Vega Stividors, the latter based in Riga’s freeport and owned by figures sanctioned by Ukraine. Since the invasion, Delfin Distribution’s glycerin imports into Russia rose 245%, and Vega Stividors’ turnover jumped from ~30 m€ in 2021 to 41 m€ in 2022. These flows exploit regulatory blind spots: while the EU has tightened controls on certain fuel additives and lubricants, raw glycerin can still enter Russia with limited end‑use scrutiny, feeding Russia’s military‑industrial base through a diffuse network of subsidiaries and holding companies.

Beyond chemicals, Bruegel’s assessment of sanctions impact notes that European fiscal and macro‑economic exposure remains secondary to energy dynamics. Russia’s current account surplus, buoyed by high hydrocarbon prices, has masked some of the longer‑term structural damage sanctions intend to inflict. The EU’s “Fortress Russia” strategy has succeeded in cutting off many direct revenue streams, but voluntary “self‑sanctioning” by firms and gradual supply‑chain rerouting via Turkey, China, and the Baltic states have diluted effectiveness. Sectors most at risk are niche chemical suppliers, specialty‑additive manufacturers, and firms with deep historical exposure to the Russian market; companies with high dependency on Russia were notably less likely to comply quickly with new restrictions, according to a 2026 Wiley study on sanction responses.

Regulatory momentum is increasing. The EU’s 14th and 17th sanction packages have expanded bans on industrial machinery, specialty chemicals, and ball bearings, while U.S. and UK measures now target secondary networks enabling Russia’s military procurement. Nevertheless, as the Bruegel paper cautions, the combination of persistent energy exports, import substitution by Russia, and gaps in dual‑use controls means that many European manufacturers—particularly in chemicals and lubricants—remain exposed, albeit often indirectly. The risk is not necessarily immediate revenue loss for most large OEMs, but rather reputational, legal, and supply‑chain disruption risks as sanctions enforcement tightens and third‑country proxies come under greater scrutiny.

In sum, European manufacturers face a fragmented exposure profile: low direct market dependence for most, but meaningful indirect vulnerability through chemical, additive, and component supply chains that are increasingly weaponized or exploited for evasion. Tightening dual‑use controls, enhancing transparency in intermediary networks, and coordinating sanctions with like‑minded partners are essential to close the loopholes that currently allow certain European‑sourced inputs to sustain Russia’s war economy.
