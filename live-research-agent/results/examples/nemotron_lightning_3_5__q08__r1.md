## Briefing

**Conflict Minerals Sourcing in Eastern Congo: A Briefing**

For over three decades, armed militias in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) have financed their operations by mining and trading coltan, gold, tin, tungsten, and cobalt—minerals collectively known as “conflict minerals.” The DRC holds an estimated $24 trillion in untapped deposits, yet this wealth has fueled war, sexual violence, and pervasive poverty rather than development. The four core conflict minerals—gold plus the 3Ts (tin, tantalum, tungsten)—are essential to global electronics supply chains, but their extraction in eastern Congo has long been dominated by armed groups who use profits to purchase weapons and perpetuate violence.

**Regulatory Response and Measurable Impact**  
In response, the United States passed Section 1502 of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act in 2010, implemented by the SEC in 2012. The law requires U.S.-listed companies to annually report whether their products contain conflict minerals from the DRC or neighboring countries and to describe due diligence measures taken. Far from forcing divestment, Dodd-Frank 1502 created a transparency lever that has produced concrete improvements. As the Enough Project notes, more than 75 percent of the world’s smelters and refiners for the four minerals now pass third‑party audits. Independent data from the International Peace Information Service (IPIS) show that by 2016, 79 percent of 3T miners in eastern Congo were working in mines where no armed group involvement had been reported— a dramatic shift from the U.N. Group of Experts’ 2010 assessment that “almost every mining deposit” in the Kivu provinces was controlled by a military group. By June 2018, 78 percent of global smelters/refiners (253 of 324) had passed independent audits under the Responsible Minerals Assurance Process, and 495 mines in eastern Congo had been validated as conflict‑free by multi‑stakeholder teams as of December 2017. This certification shift has created a two‑tier market: untraceable 3T minerals command significantly lower prices than verified conflict‑free minerals, making the illicit trade less lucrative for armed actors.

**Persistent Challenges and Livelihood Realities**  
Progress has not been uniform. The transition from a conflict‑driven to a conflict‑free mining economy has disrupted artisanal miners’ livelihoods. Livelihood‑support provisions that were proposed in early drafts of the conflict‑minerals legislation were omitted from the final Dodd‑Frank law, leaving many miners without alternative income sources. The Enough Project and Congolese civil society have repeatedly called for complementary livelihood projects—microfinance, women’s access to mining, formalization through artisanal mining zones, and health‑safety capacity building—to ease these hardships. Gold remains the most lucrative and easily smuggled resource, and the U.N. Group of Experts’ 2016 report still identifies it as providing “the most significant financial benefit to armed groups.” Moreover, some companies and commentators continue to argue that the law has caused unintended negative consequences, but a 2018 Radboud University study found such claims rely on outdated data.

**Congolese Voices and Ongoing Risks**  
Congolese leaders and civil society have been vocal supporters of the reform. Over 100 Congolese civil‑society groups and the Ministry of Mines have written to the SEC urging that Section 1502 not be weakened, arguing that its suspension would “lead to increased violence and conflict‑minerals smuggling.” Nobel‑Peace‑Prize nominee Dr. Denis Mukwege has stated that “a conflict‑free minerals industry would greatly benefit the people of Congo.” Open letters from North‑Kivu civil‑society groups credit the law with more than 220 certified green mining sites, over 300 trained mining police, and a reduction in crime and human‑rights violations, including sexual violence. Indeed, sexual violence remains a deliberate tactic used by armed groups to control mineral‑rich areas, with women and girls targeted in mining zones to terrorize communities and facilitate exploitation.

**Conclusion**  
Conflict‑minerals sourcing in eastern Congo sits at the intersection of natural‑resource wealth, armed violence, and global supply‑chain demand. Dodd‑Frank 1502 and related international mechanisms have undeniably increased transparency, expanded certified conflict‑free mines, and depressed the market price of untraceable minerals—key steps toward breaking armed‑group financing. Yet the law’s voluntary nature, gaps in livelihood support, and the continued centrality of gold to illicit flows mean that significant challenges remain. Stronger regulation, expanded due‑diligence support for artisanal miners, and sustained civil‑society engagement are essential to ensure that Congo’s mineral wealth benefits its people rather than fuels enduring conflict.
