## Briefing

Since 2020, Hong Kong's standing as a preeminent international financial centre has undergone significant reconfiguration, shaped by geopolitical turbulence, public health disruption, and shifting market dynamics. Prior to 2020, the city consistently ranked third globally in the Z/Yen Group’s Global Financial Centres Index, behind only New York and London, and served as the primary offshore renminbi hub and gateway for China’s capital markets [https://www.cato.org/cato-journal/winter-2021/financial-development-hong-kong-china-hayekian-perspective].

The triple shock of the national security law enacted in June 2020, strict COVID-19 lockdowns lasting nearly three years, and the United States’ 2020 decision to terminate Hong Kong’s separate customs territory status under the Hong Kong Autonomy Act combined to erode confidence. In March 2020, the city dropped to sixth place in the GFCI rankings, reflecting concerns over legal predictability and capital freedom [same Cato source]. Over the following years, annual Hang Seng Index declines of 3–15% [https://www.thinkchina.sg/economy/hong-kong-makes-comeback-international-financial-centre], a slide in global IPO rankings to sixth place by 2023 [same ThinkChina source], and the exodus of regional headquarters—falling to an 11-year low in 2023—signalled a turbulent mid-decade period. Economists note that pandemic-related border closures, rather than politics alone, drove much of the expatriate outflow, though the national security law undeniably heightened investor caution, particularly in Western capitals [same ThinkChina source].

By 2024, however, stabilisation emerged. The number of regional headquarters based in Hong Kong recorded its first annual increase in five years, and in March 2025 the city reclaimed third place in the GFCI with an overall rating of 760, up 11 points and narrowing the gap with London and New York [https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202503/20/P2025032000391.htm]. Financial Secretary Paul Chan reported that total bank deposits reached HK$18 trillion in 2025 and net capital inflows for registered funds exceeded US$44 billion, up nearly 300% year-on-year, driven in part by high-profile listings such as CATL’s HK$41 billion IPO in May 2025 [same ThinkChina source].

The nature of Hong Kong’s financial role has nonetheless shifted. Mainland Chinese companies now account for approximately 70% of the Hang Seng Index’s weighting, up ten percentage points since 2021, and nearly 45% of daily trading volume comes from mainland investors [https://www.thinkchina.sg/economy/hong-kong-makes-comeback-international-financial-centre]. This reflects a broader realignment: Hong Kong remains deeply integrated with China’s economy, even as it leverages its legal framework, tax regime, and free capital flow to attract cross-border capital seeking China exposure. Geopolitical risks persist—US pension funds have excluded Hong Kong and mainland assets, and technology-sector restrictions continue—but the city’s recovery in 2025 suggests resilience. As Beijing expands its international dispute mediation presence in Hong Kong and cross-border IPO pipelines rebuild, the city’s status as an international financial centre is redefined rather than extinguished: more China-centric, more geopolitically sensitive, but still firmly within the top tier globally [https://www.hkma.gov.hk/eng/key-functions/international-financial-centre/hong-kong-as-an-international-financial-centre/].
