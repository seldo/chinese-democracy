## Briefing

Since 2020, Hong Kong’s standing as an international financial centre has undergone a turbulent but ultimately resilient transformation, shaped by domestic unrest, the imposition of national security legislation, and shifting geopolitical dynamics. 

In the first half of 2020, Hong Kong’s Global Financial Centres Index (GFCI) ranking fell from third to sixth place, reflecting the dual shock of 2019’s pro-democracy protests and the COVID-19 pandemic. A CNBC report from July 2020 noted that the territory had “already dropped from third to sixth place from September 2019 to March 2020,” overtaken by Tokyo, Shanghai, and Singapore, with investor confidence undermined by street violence and economic contraction. The subsequent passage of the National Security Law in June 2020 intensified global concerns about the erosion of Hong Kong’s distinct legal framework, with the BBC warning that the legislation “raises the stakes for foreign capital” and could accelerate an “anywhere but China” mindset among Western investors. 

Despite these headwinds, the centre’s trajectory has proven more durable than early forecasts suggested. By February 2022, Hong Kong had reclaimed third place in the GFCI, as cited by Hong Kong’s Constitutional and Mainland Affairs Bureau, and subsequent editions have consistently placed it third globally (765 points in GFCI 39, March 2026) while retaining the top spot in Asia Pacific. The Hong Kong government’s own 2026 release affirmed that the city’s “overall rating increased further by one point to 765, maintaining third place globally and first place in Asia Pacific.” This recovery reflects the continued advantage of Hong Kong’s currency peg to the US dollar, its low-tax regime, and its role as the primary offshore yuan and dollar funding hub for mainland Chinese corporates seeking international capital.

Structurally, however, the landscape has shifted. Competition from Singapore and Shanghai has intensified, and some multinational firms have expanded regional hubs elsewhere to mitigate political risk. The 2024 Article 23 legislation reignited scrutiny, with the German Chamber of Commerce warning of “higher compliance costs” and broader doubts about the “distinctness” of Hong Kong’s common-law system. American business sentiment remains mixed: a 2024 Guardian piece noted that tighter security laws “may sap yet more foreign investment,” while the Hong Kong General Chamber of Commerce counters that the laws will “make Hong Kong a safer destination.” 

Today, Hong Kong remains the world’s third-ranked financial centre and Asia’s pre-eminent link to Western capital markets, particularly for dollar-denominated financing and initial public offerings. Yet its status is no longer taken for granted. The centre has weathered the 2020 storm largely through the strength of its institutional infrastructure and market connectivity, but it now operates in a more contested environment where geopolitical alignment, regulatory transparency, and the preservation of rule-of-law perceptions are decisive factors in sustaining its competitive edge.
