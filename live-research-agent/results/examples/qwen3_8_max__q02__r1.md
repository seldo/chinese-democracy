## Briefing

**Briefing: Labor Conditions in Xinjiang's Cotton Industry**

Xinjiang produces roughly 85–90% of China's cotton and about a fifth of global supply, making labor conditions there a major concern for international apparel supply chains.

**Allegations of forced labor.** A body of research alleges state-imposed forced labor tied to cotton production. The Australian Strategic Policy Institute's March 2020 report "Uyghurs for Sale" documented the transfer of more than 80,000 Uyghur workers from Xinjiang to factories across China, many linked to "vocational training" and detention facilities, supplying at least 82 global brands (https://www.aspi.org.au/report/uyghurs-sale/). Sheffield Hallam University's November 2021 "Laundering Cotton" report found Xinjiang cotton is frequently obscured in global supply chains through blending, transshipment, and intermediary processing (https://www.shu.ac.uk/news/all-articles/latest-news/laundering-cotton-report). A December 2020 investigation reported hundreds of thousands of ethnic minority laborers being mobilized for hand-picking cotton under coercive conditions (https://www.rte.ie/news/world/2020/1215/1184487-uighurs-china/).

**International findings.** The UN Human Rights Office's August 2022 assessment concluded that "serious human rights violations" had been committed in Xinjiang, potentially amounting to crimes against humanity, citing discriminatory detention and employment-related coercion (https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/country-reports/ohchr-assessment-human-rights-concerns-xinjiang-uyghur-autonomous-region). UN experts in January 2026 warned of a "persistent pattern" of alleged state-imposed forced labor involving Uyghur, Tibetan, and other minorities (https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2026/01/un-experts-alarmed-reports-forced-labour-uyghur-tibetan-and-other-minorities). A 2025 U.S. State Department report continued to flag forced labor risks in Xinjiang's cotton sector (https://www.ecotextile.com/2025081859146/news/social-compliance-and-csr/report-flags-ongoing-forced-labour-risks-in-chinas-cotton-industry/).

**Policy responses.** The U.S. banned all Xinjiang cotton and tomato products in January 2021, then enacted the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA), effective June 2022, which presumes all goods made in whole or part in Xinjiang are prohibited unless importers prove otherwise (https://www.cbp.gov/trade/forced-labor/UFLPA). As of August 2025, Customs and Border Protection had detained 16,755 shipments worth nearly $3.7 billion, and 144 entities had been added to the UFLPA Entity List; analysis indicates the law has shifted sourcing away from Xinjiang cotton toward U.S., Indian, and Australian alternatives (https://www.csis.org/analysis/assessing-impact-uyghur-forced-labor-prevention-act-after-three-years). The EU's forced labor ban is set to take effect in 2027.

**China's position.** Beijing denies all forced labor allegations, describing labor transfer programs as voluntary poverty alleviation and employment support. State media report that workers in Xinjiang reject the accusations as fabricated (https://news.cgtn.com/news/2021-09-08/VHJhbnNjcmlwdDU4NDAx/index.html). Chinese sources also emphasize rapid mechanization: official figures put the machine-harvesting rate above 80–90%, reducing reliance on manual pickers (https://english.news.cn/20231024/0b3837e0ccf946219be835a518a210fc/c.html). China has retaliated against brands that distanced themselves from Xinjiang cotton, triggering consumer boycotts, and characterizes the allegations as politically motivated (https://www.latimes.com/opinion/story/2021-05-28/china-uighurs-cotton-forced-labor).

**Assessment.** Evidence of coercive labor practices is extensive but contested; independent verification inside Xinjiang remains extremely difficult, and mechanization has changed the sector's labor profile.
