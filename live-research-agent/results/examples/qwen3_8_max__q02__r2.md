## Briefing

# Labor Conditions in Xinjiang's Cotton Industry: A Briefing

## Scale and Context
Xinjiang produces the vast majority of China's cotton, a significant share of global supply. Labor conditions there have become one of the most contested issues in international trade and human rights, with sharply divergent accounts from researchers, governments, and Chinese authorities.

## Allegations and Research Findings
Multiple independent studies allege systemic coercion. The Australian Strategic Policy Institute's *Uyghurs for Sale* (2020) documented the mass transfer of Uyghur and other minority workers from Xinjiang, estimating at least 80,000 Uyghurs were sent to factories across China under potentially abusive "labor transfer" programs (https://www.aspi.org.au/report/uyghurs-sale/). Adrian Zenz's peer-reviewed study in *Communist and Post-Communist Studies* (2023) traces "systemic state-sponsored coercive labor" in Xinjiang's cotton harvest, arguing coercion persisted into 2022 (https://online.ucpress.edu/cpcs/article/56/2/1/196124/Coercive-Labor-in-the-Cotton-Harvest-in-the; https://www.just-style.com/news/forced-labour-in-xinjiang-cotton-picking-will-persist-says-expert/). Sheffield Hallam University's *Laundering Cotton* report (2021), led by Laura Murphy, found Xinjiang cotton is obscured in global supply chains and linked to major retail brands (https://www.shu.ac.uk/news/all-articles/latest-news/laundering-cotton-report). The UN Human Rights Office's 2022 assessment raised serious concerns about detention and discriminatory labor practices in the region (https://fashionunited.uk/news/business/what-to-know-about-the-un-s-xinjiang-human-rights-report/2022090264980). Walk Free's Global Slavery Index similarly cites evidence of forced labor since 2018 (https://www.walkfree.org/global-slavery-index/country-studies/china/).

## Chinese Government Rebuttal
Beijing rejects the allegations as fabricated. Officials and local farmers say workers participate voluntarily and are paid (https://en.people.cn/n3/2021/1112/c90000-9918777.html). A central counterargument is mechanization: Chinese authorities state the mechanized cotton-harvesting rate reached nearly 70 percent in 2020 (https://ca.china-embassy.gov.cn/eng/zt/xingjiang2021/202104/t20210401_9029489.htm), rising above 85 percent by 2023 and to roughly 94 percent overall mechanization (https://www.iprcc.org/article/4FQRkrzqclP). A USDA 2025 report confirms planting mechanization hit 100 percent in 2024 (https://apps.fas.usda.gov/newgainapi/api/Report/DownloadReportByFileName?fileName=Cotton%20and%20Products%20Annual_Beijing_China%20-%20People%27s%20Republic%20of_CH2025-0087.pdf). State media argue Western reports are "full of holes" (https://news.cgtn.com/news/2020-12-16/CGP-report-on-Xinjiang-labor-is-full-of-holes-WgHHrIdSc8/index.html), and China told the ILO its employment measures are lawful and voluntary (https://normlex.ilo.org/dyn/nrmlx_en/f?p=1000:13101:0::NO:13101:P13101_COMMENT_ID:4465597).

## Policy and Supply Chain Responses
The U.S. Customs and Border Protection issued a Withhold Release Order on Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps cotton in December 2020 over prison-labor indicators (https://www.globalcompliancenews.com/2021/01/03/us-cbp-issues-wro-on-cotton-products-made-by-xinjiang-production-and-construction-corps-using-prison-labor-03122020/), then Congress passed the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act, creating a rebuttable presumption that Xinjiang goods are made with forced labor (https://www.csis.org/analysis/responding-egregious-abuses-xinjiang-latest-state-play). The UK introduced parallel measures (https://www.vogue.com/article/uk-xinjiang-forced-labur-new-measures), and follow-up research traces apparel with possible Xinjiang links entering Europe (https://www.business-humanrights.org/it/ultime-notizie/report-finds-substantial-volume-of-apparel-with-possible-links-to-uyghur-forced-labour-is-entering-the-eu/).

## Assessment
Independent researchers and Western governments describe a pattern of coercive state-organized labor, while Beijing points to mechanization and voluntary employment. Restricted access to Xinjiang makes independent verification difficult, so the dispute remains unresolved and highly politicized.
