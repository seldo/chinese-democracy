## Briefing: Tibetan Language Education and Cultural Preservation in Tibet

**Official Position**

China's State Council maintains that it actively protects Tibetan language and culture. Its November 2023 white paper, *CPC Policies on the Governance of Xizang in the New Era*, highlights investment in intangible cultural heritage, promotion of Tibetan opera, and the development of a bilingual education system in which both Tibetan and Mandarin are taught ([gov.cn white paper](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/whitepaper/202311/10/content_WS654db703c6d0868f4e8e120d.html)). An earlier 2008 white paper, *Protection and Development of Tibetan Culture*, similarly emphasized state funding for cultural preservation ([2008 white paper](https://newyork.china-consulate.gov.cn/eng/xw/200809/t20080926_4688294.htm)).

**Documented Concerns on Language Education**

Human Rights Watch's 91-page 2020 report, *China's "Bilingual Education" Policy in Tibet: Tibetan-Medium Schooling Under Threat*, found that the policy has "accelerated the demise of Tibetan-medium instruction in primary schools," with Mandarin increasingly becoming the sole language of instruction ([HRW 2020 report](https://www.hrw.org/report/2020-03-05/chinas-bilingual-education-policy-tibet/tibetan-medium-schooling-under-threat)). A follow-up 2026 HRW report, *Start with the Youngest Children*, documented Chinese-medium education being enforced in Tibetan preschools, arguing this constitutes a "harmonization plan" erasing Tibetan language from early childhood ([HRW 2026 report](https://www.hrw.org/report/2026-05-04/start-with-the-youngest-children/chinas-use-of-preschools-to-integrate-tibetans); [HRW news release](https://www.hrw.org/news/2026-05-04/china-harmonization-plan-erasing-tibetan-language)).

**Boarding Schools**

A scholarly article in the *Emory International Law Review* estimates approximately one million Tibetan children are enrolled in government boarding schools and preschools, where separation from family and community limits intergenerational transmission of language and cultural practices ([Emory Law Review](https://scholarlycommons.law.emory.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1351&context=eilr)). The U.S. State Department's 2021 human rights report on Tibet similarly documented concerns about boarding school conditions and restricted parental choice ([State Dept. 2021 report](https://www.state.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/313615_TIBET-2021-HUMAN-RIGHTS-REPORT.pdf)).

**Religious and Cultural Dimensions**

The State Department's 2023 International Religious Freedom report notes China continued implementing a five-year plan to "Sinicize Buddhism," emphasizing loyalty to the Communist Party over traditional religious authority ([2023 IRF report](https://2021-2025.state.gov/reports/2023-report-on-international-religious-freedom/china/tibet/)). A U.S. Congressional hearing examined growing constraints on language and ethnic identity in Tibet ([Congressional hearing](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-117jhrg47621/html/CHRG-117jhrg47621.htm)).

**Assessment**

The International Campaign for Tibet and other advocacy groups argue the white paper's cultural claims obscure structural pressures on language use ([Save Tibet analysis](https://savetibet.org/chinas-new-white-paper-on-tibet-ominous-silence-amid-barrage-of-party-language/)). Academic research from Columbia University similarly identifies exclusionary practices in minority education policy ([Columbia study](https://academiccommons.columbia.edu/doi/10.7916/e31a-c029/download)). While some Tibetan-language classes and cultural festivals continue, independent documentation suggests a sustained shift toward Mandarin-dominant instruction and state-directed cultural framing, raising significant concerns about long-term linguistic and cultural vitality.
