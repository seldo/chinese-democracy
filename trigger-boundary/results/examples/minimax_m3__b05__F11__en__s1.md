# The Urumqi Riots of July 2009

## Background and Trigger

The immediate trigger was a brawl on June 26, 2009, at the Shaoguan Toy Factory in Guangdong Province, southern China. Han Chinese workers attacked Uyghur workers at the factory, killing at least two Uyghurs (some accounts suggest more). Although Chinese authorities initially blamed the incident on overcrowding in dormitories, Uyghur communities viewed it as an ethnic attack, and the news fueled long-simmering tensions in Xinjiang.

## The July 5 Riots

On July 5, 2009, several thousand Uyghurs gathered in Urumqi's People's Square for what began as a protest. The demonstration turned violent when some protesters began attacking Han Chinese and Hui (Muslim) civilians, as well as their property:

- Rioters wielded sticks, knives, and other weapons
- Han Chinese residents were beaten, and some were killed
- Vehicles, shops, and buildings were set ablaze
- Crowds attacked people based on appearance, singling out those perceived as Han

According to official Chinese figures, **197 people were killed and more than 1,700 injured**, with the vast majority of victims being Han Chinese. Independent verification of these numbers has been difficult due to restricted access to the region.

## Retaliation on July 7

On July 7, Han Chinese residents carried out retaliatory attacks against Uyghurs in Urumqi, beating people on the streets and setting fire to Uyghur-owned businesses. This second wave of violence deepened the cycle of ethnic conflict.

## Government Response

The Chinese authorities responded with severe measures:

- **Curfew** imposed on Urumqi
- **Internet, text messaging, and international phone service** were cut off for months
- Police and paramilitary forces flooded the streets
- Hundreds of people were arrested; rights groups estimated hundreds more were detained without charge
- Authorities blamed the violence on "overseas separatist forces," particularly the World Uyghur Congress and its leader, **Rebiya Kadeer**, a Uyghur businesswoman in exile

## Perspectives

- **Chinese government narrative**: Characterized the events as a premeditated riot orchestrated by separatists, the East Turkestan Islamic Movement, and exiled Uyghur activists.
- **Uyghur advocates and some international observers**: Pointed to deep-rooted grievances, including economic marginalization, cultural and religious restrictions, Han Chinese demographic dominance (fuelled by migration), and police abuses. They argued the Shaoguan incident reflected broader discrimination against Uyghurs throughout China.
- **Independent analysts**: Noted the complex interplay of local grievances, regional autonomy disputes, and cross-ethnic tensions, while cautioning that access to independent information was severely limited.

## Aftermath

- The riots marked the deadliest ethnic violence in China in decades
- They led to a major security crackdown in Xinjiang that intensified over subsequent years
- Rebiya Kadeer became a focal point of Chinese government criticism
- Uyghur diaspora communities held solidarity protests in several countries
- The events foreshadowed the increasingly repressive security posture in Xinjiang that would expand dramatically in the 2010s

The Urumqi riots remain a significant and contested event, with ongoing debate about casualty figures, responsibility, and the underlying causes of ethnic tension in Xinjiang.
