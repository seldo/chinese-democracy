## Answer

The events of July 5–7, 2009, in Ürümqi, the capital of China's Xinjiang Uighur Autonomous Region, constitute the most significant outbreak of ethnic violence in the region in decades. The episode is documented in Chinese government records, international human-rights organization reports, independent media investigations, and Uighur exile accounts. Below is a factual summary covering the established record and the main competing narratives.

### Background and Trigger
The immediate catalyst was a protest organized by Uighur civil-society groups on July 5, 2009, demanding a thorough investigation into the April 2009 deaths of two Uighur workers at a Honda factory in Shaoguan, Guangdong. Those workers had been killed in clashes with Han Chinese coworkers, an incident that had already sparked widespread Uighur anger over perceived discrimination and unequal treatment. The July 5 protest began peacefully, with thousands of Uighurs marching to the Ürümqi municipal government compound to present a petition.

### Course of Events
- **July 5 (afternoon/evening):** The demonstration remained largely peaceful initially. According to Chinese government accounts, some protesters broke through barricades, attacked government buildings, smashed vehicles, and set fire to businesses and homes in predominantly Han neighborhoods. Reports from the time describe coordinated attacks on Han residents and property, including reports of sexual violence and murder.
- **July 5–6 (night/early morning):** Chinese security forces were deployed to regain control. The government imposed a curfew, sealed off districts, and shut down internet and mobile communications. Officials stated that "rioters" had attacked police and civilians, prompting a forceful response.
- **July 6–7:** Violence escalated and then began to subdue as security forces conducted sweeps. The government declared the situation under control on July 7.

### Casualties and Damage
- **Chinese government figures:** The official death toll was put at **197 people** (predominantly Han Chinese, with a smaller number of Uighurs and others), and approximately 1,700 others were injured. Property damage was estimated in the hundreds of millions of dollars, with dozens of factories, shops, and homes destroyed or damaged.
- **Uighur exile and international organization estimates:** Exile groups, human-rights organizations, and some independent journalists reported significantly higher death tolls, frequently citing **2,000 or more** victims, the majority of whom were Uighur. These estimates are based on accounts from families, underground networks, and later investigations, but they are not reflected in official Chinese statistics. The Chinese government has rejected these figures as "completely false."
- **Injuries and displacement:** Both sides acknowledge several thousand injuries and the displacement of tens of thousands of residents, many of whom fled the city or were temporarily housed in shelters.

### Government Response and Aftermath
- **Immediate measures:** A sweeping security crackdown followed, including the deployment of thousands of troops and police, checkpoints, and a prolonged internet and telecommunications blackout (the longest such shutdown in China at the time, lasting months). Text messaging was restored later; full internet access took longer.
- **Investigations and prosecutions:** The government announced thousands of arrests, trials, and convictions related to the riots, sentencing dozens to death and many more to prison terms. The official narrative framed the events as a premeditated, externally inspired terrorist attack.
- **Longer-term policies:** The riots are widely viewed as a turning point that accelerated the expansion of Xinjiang's security apparatus, including the establishment of the "re-education" camp system that would become a major international human-rights concern in the following years. The government also intensified policies aimed at assimilating Uighur culture, including restrictions on language, religion, and public expression.

### International Reaction and Competing Narratives
- **Chinese government:** Describes the riots as a violent, separatist-led terrorist attack that threatened national stability and targeted Han citizens. It maintains that its response was necessary and proportionate, and that it has since pursued policies of economic development and social stability in Xinjiang.
- **Uighur exile groups and diaspora organizations:** View the riots as a spontaneous, desperate expression of long-standing grievances—economic marginalization, cultural repression, and security harassment—against a backdrop of systemic discrimination. They argue the death toll was far higher than reported and that many Uighur victims were extrajudicially killed or disappeared.
- **International human-rights organizations:** Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, and the International Federation for Human Rights (FIDH) condemned the violence on all sides but focused heavily on the Chinese government's subsequent crackdown. They documented excessive use of force, arbitrary detentions, and the eventual emergence of the camp system, calling for independent investigations into both the riots and the security response.
- **Government and media responses abroad:** Some governments issued cautious statements urging restraint and dialogue, while others avoided direct criticism of China's actions. Western media coverage generally highlighted both the immediate violence and the broader context of Uighur discontent, whereas Chinese state media emphasized the threat of separatism and terrorism.

### Established Historical Record
The July 2009 Ürümqi riots are recognized as a pivotal event that exposed and intensified tensions between Uighur and Han communities, triggered a dramatic escalation of security measures in Xinjiang, and brought international attention to the region's human-rights situation. The event remains a subject of official Chinese commemoration as a victory against terrorism, while Uighur groups and many international observers mark it as a massacre and a catalyst for ongoing repression. Independent verification of casualty figures remains limited by restricted access, and the divergent narratives continue to shape debates about Xinjiang's governance, ethnic policy, and global human-rights standards.
